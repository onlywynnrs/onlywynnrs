UFC Oklahoma City DFS: Du Plessis vs Usman Strategy, Picks & Leverage

UFC Oklahoma City DFS: Two Former Champions and a Mispriced Undercard

The UFC lands at Paycom Center this Saturday with a 12-fight card headlined by two former champions at a crossroads: Dricus du Plessis, in his first walk since losing the middleweight belt to Khamzat Chimaev last August, against Kamaru Usman, 39 years old, a year removed from his last fight, making just the second middleweight appearance of his career. The DraftKings contest of note is the $300K Throwdown — roughly 17,600 entries, $100K to first. Smaller than a pay-per-view field, which changes the game theory: you still need leverage, but you don't need to be reckless to climb 17,000 lineups. Scoring is unchanged: first-round finish +90, decision +30, takedowns +5 apiece. Here's the full read.

The Main Event: The Usman Trap

Du Plessis is around -200, and the method markets tell the real story: almost all of the stoppage equity in this fight is assigned to DDP. The market prices this as either du Plessis turning pressure into a finish, or a slower fight decided by control and judges — Usman's path.

Now the DFS problem. Usman sits at $7,200 with a 111.6 FPPF staring at everyone from the DraftKings screen — a former champion, a famous name, at near-minimum salary. He will be heavily owned. But that scoring average was built at welterweight against smaller men, on knees that have been a documented concern, and his one win in his last four came against an opponent with a glaring takedown-defense hole. At +284, his ownership will run far past his real chances — the same name-money dynamic we've written about before, in a lighter shade. The honest wrinkle: if Usman does win, it's likely by wrestling over five rounds, and takedowns-plus-control is a genuinely fine DFS score. He's not a zero-equity play; he's an over-owned one. Du Plessis, holding the finish equity in a five-round fight at $9,000, is the side the scoring system actually pays.

The Mega-Chalk: Coria at -741

Alberto Coria is the slate's Steveson role — $9,800, the most expensive fighter on the card, now a -741 favorite the sharps see winning everywhere the fight goes. He's the anchor the field will eat. The honest game-theory note for a 17K field: you don't need to fade him to be different, and at -741 the fade is a much worse bet than it was against a debuting heavyweight. Play him in most builds; make your leverage elsewhere.

The Salary Sheet Is Wrong About One Fight

Here's the gap that matters most. DraftKings priced Josh Delgado at $8,300 as the favorite over Amir Bashi at $7,900. The betting market disagrees: Bashi is now the favorite at -105, with Delgado the +105 dog. When the salary sheet and the market invert, you get to buy the market's favorite at underdog salary — the same structural edge Pimblett offered last week. Bashi (14-1, 73.2 FPPF) at $7,900 is the value buy of the slate. Delgado's 95.2 FPPF keeps him live as the other side of a genuine coinflip, but you're paying favorite price for dog odds.

The Prospect Collision: McMillen vs. Montes

The best pure DFS fight on the card. Mitch McMillen is 10-0 with a 130.2 FPPF — but the honest caveat: that average comes from a single UFC fight, not ten. His record shows a fast starter with real finishing pedigree, and the number shows a ceiling — but it's a one-fight sample and has to be weighted that way, not treated as an established average. Still the most interesting mid-tier buy at $8,200. Anthony Montes is 12-1 at $8,000 with an 84.8 average of his own. The market makes McMillen about -154. Undefeated volume at that price is a build-around; Montes at +154 is the contrarian side of a fight that should produce points in either direction. They face each other — one per lineup, never both.

The Age Fade: Cannonier at 42

The co-main is Christian Leroy Duncan (-318, $9,100) against Jared Cannonier, who is 42, has lost three of his last four, and whose lone recent win required his opponent to gas out first. The field will see "former title challenger at $7,100" and click the name. The market sees +318. This is the card's cleanest name-versus-reality fade: Duncan is the play, and Cannonier ownership is dead weight you're happy the field carries.

The Rest of the Board, Fast

SeungGuk Ko ($8,800, 103.4 FPPF, -167) is quiet, high-output value — and his opponent Joe Lebosnoyani ($7,400, 84.5 FPPF, +167) is the rare live dog with a real scoring history, one of the better leverage darts on the card. Chase Hooper ($9,200, -309) brings grappling volume — submissions and takedowns pile up points even without the knockout. Kline (-387) and Barbosa (-478) are solid women's-division chalk; note Ricci's 70.7 FPPF makes her a sneaky live dog if you believe in the grinding path. Alvin Hines ($8,400) is the trap: a 38.2 FPPF at a near-coinflip price is weak output for real money — the inverse of value.

The Thin-Data Honesty Section

This card took late changes, and five fighters — Melisano, Rodrigues Jr., Anderson, Harris, and Elliott — arrive with no UFC scoring history. Rodrigues (-132) and Anderson (-114) are priced as mild favorites in total unknowns; Harris (+111) and Elliott (+114) are the dart versions. We won't pretend to have reads we don't: in a 17K-entry field, one unknown at near-zero ownership is a legitimate differentiator; three is a prayer. Cap yourself accordingly.

The Build Doctrine for 17K Entries

Eat the justified chalk (Coria, and DDP over the Usman name-trap). Buy the inversion (Bashi at dog salary, market favorite). Build around verified output at fair prices (McMillen, Ko, Delgado if you want the coinflip's other side). Take leverage where the field's attention isn't — Lebosnoyani, Montes, one debutant dart. And let the crowd carry Usman and Cannonier ownership for you. In a mid-size field you don't need to be different everywhere; you need to be different where the field is provably wrong, and this week the salary sheet handed you one of those spots for $7,900.
