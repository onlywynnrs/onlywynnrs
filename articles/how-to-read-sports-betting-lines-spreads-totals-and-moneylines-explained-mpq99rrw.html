How to Read Sports Betting Lines: Spreads, Totals, and Moneylines Explained

Understanding the Three Main Betting Line Types

When you first look at a sportsbook, the rows of numbers can feel overwhelming. But every bet you'll encounter falls into three basic categories: spreads, totals, and moneylines. Once you understand how each works, you'll be able to evaluate opportunities across any sport.

The moneyline is the simplest. You're picking who wins, period. A favorite might be listed at -180, meaning you'd risk $180 to win $100. An underdog at +150 means you'd win $150 on a $100 bet. The bigger the number, the bigger the perceived gap between competitors.

Point spreads level the playing field by giving the underdog a head start. If a team is favored by -6.5, they need to win by 7 or more for your bet to cash. Take the underdog at +6.5, and they can lose by 6 or fewer (or win outright) and you still win your bet. Both sides typically pay around -110, meaning you risk $110 to win $100.

Totals (also called over/unders) ignore who wins entirely. The sportsbook sets a combined score, and you bet whether the actual total goes over or under that number. A total of 215.5 means you're wagering on whether both sides combine for 216+ points or 215 or fewer.

Reading the Juice and Finding Value

Those numbers next to each bet—usually -110, -115, or similar—represent the juice or vigoreous, the sportsbook's commission. Standard juice is -110 on both sides of a spread or total, meaning the book takes roughly 4.5% of handle as their cut.

When you see -110 on both sides, the sportsbook is saying these outcomes are equally likely in their view. But when the juice gets unbalanced—say -105 on one side and -115 on the other—that's the book trying to attract money to the less popular side while still hanging the same spread.

This is where line shopping becomes critical. Different sportsbooks often post different lines on the same event. One book might have a spread at -5.5 (-110) while another has -6 (-108). That half-point difference can be the margin between winning and losing, and over time, consistently getting the best number available compounds into significant value.

Sharp bettors track closing line value—the difference between the number they bet and where the line closes. If you take a team at +3 and the line moves to +1 by game time, you've captured value regardless of whether your bet wins. Over large samples, consistently beating the closing line correlates strongly with long-term profitability.

Spotting Reverse Line Movement

One of the most valuable skills you can develop is recognizing when lines move against public betting patterns. This phenomenon, called reverse line movement, happens when the majority of bets come in on one side but the line moves in the opposite direction.

For example, if 75% of bets are on the favorite at -6 but the line moves to -5.5, that tells you something important: sharp money or large wagers are hitting the underdog, and the sportsbook respects that action enough to adjust despite lopsided ticket counts.

Sportsbooks don't want balanced action—they want to set accurate lines. When respected bettors or syndicates place significant wagers, books move lines to adjust their exposure, even if it means taking on more risk from casual bettors on the other side.

You can find betting percentages on various tracking sites. Compare those percentages to line movement throughout the day. When they contradict each other, you've identified a potential opportunity to follow the sharp money.

Bankroll Management and Unit Sizing

Understanding betting lines matters little if you're not managing your bankroll properly. The most common recommendation is the fixed unit approach: decide on a unit size (typically 1-2% of your total bankroll) and bet in multiples of that unit based on confidence level.

If your bankroll is $2,000 and your unit is 2% ($40), a standard bet is one unit. A play you feel more confident about might be 1.5 or 2 units, but avoid going beyond 3-4 units even on your strongest convictions. Variance affects even the best handicappers, and oversizing bets is how winning bettors go broke.

As your bankroll grows or shrinks, adjust your unit size accordingly. This protects you during cold streaks and allows you to capitalize during hot runs without risking too much of your stack on any single outcome.

The goal isn't to win every bet—it's to find positive expected value opportunities and execute them with proper sizing over time. Small edges compound when you're disciplined about bankroll management and consistently getting the best available lines.
