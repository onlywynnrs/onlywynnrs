How to Read Betting Lines: Spreads, Totals, and Moneylines Explained

Understanding the Three Main Types of Betting Lines

If you're new to sports betting in 2026, walking up to a sportsbook board or opening a betting app can feel overwhelming. Those numbers, plus signs, and minus signs might look like a foreign language. The good news? Learning to read betting lines is simpler than it seems, and once you understand the basics, you'll be able to evaluate any wager with confidence.

Every bet you place falls into one of three main categories: spreads, totals, or moneylines. Each serves a different purpose and offers unique advantages depending on the matchup and your betting strategy. Let's break down exactly what each means and how to read them.

Point Spreads: Leveling the Playing Field

The point spread is the most popular betting option in football and basketball. It's designed to even out the action between a favorite and an underdog by giving one team a head start and the other a handicap.

When you see a line like Team A -7.5 vs Team B +7.5, the minus sign indicates the favorite, while the plus sign marks the underdog. If you bet on Team A -7.5, they need to win by at least 8 points for your bet to cash. If you take Team B +7.5, they can lose by 7 points or fewer (or win outright) and you still win your bet.

The number next to the spread (usually -110) represents the juice or vig—the commission the sportsbook charges. At -110, you need to risk $110 to win $100. Shopping for the best lines across different sportsbooks can save you significant money over time, as spreads and juice can vary.

Spread betting is ideal when there's a clear favorite but you want more balanced odds than a moneyline would offer. The key is evaluating whether the favorite can cover that specific number, not just whether they'll win.

Moneylines: Straight-Up Winner

Moneyline betting is the simplest wager in sports betting—you're just picking who will win the game, period. No points, no spreads, just the final result.

Moneyline odds reflect how much you need to bet to win $100 (for favorites) or how much you'd win on a $100 bet (for underdogs). Here's how to read them:

A favorite at -180 means you must bet $180 to win $100. The larger the minus number, the bigger the favorite. An underdog at +160 means a $100 bet wins you $160. The larger the plus number, the bigger the underdog.

Moneylines are especially popular in baseball and hockey, where games are often decided by one or two runs or goals. They're also common in combat sports where there are only two possible winners. The tradeoff is simple: betting favorites requires more risk for smaller returns, while underdogs offer bigger payouts but lower win probability.

When evaluating moneyline value, consider whether the odds accurately reflect each side's true chances of winning. Sometimes a heavy favorite is worth the risk, other times an underdog at plus money offers genuine value.

Totals (Over/Under): Predicting Combined Scoring

Totals betting, also called over/under, doesn't require you to pick a winner at all. Instead, you're betting on whether the combined score of both teams will be over or under a number set by the sportsbook.

If a total is set at 215.5 points in a basketball game, you can bet the Over if you think both teams will combine for 216 or more points, or the Under if you think they'll combine for 215 or fewer. The half-point ensures there's no push (tie).

Totals betting requires analyzing pace of play, offensive and defensive efficiency, weather conditions (for outdoor sports), and situational factors. Two defensive-minded teams might play a slower pace leading to fewer points, while high-powered offenses could sail over a total even if it's set high.

Like spreads, totals usually come with -110 juice on both sides, though this can shift based on betting action. Many experienced bettors prefer totals because you're not picking sides—you're simply evaluating the game's flow and scoring environment.

Putting It All Together

Reading betting lines becomes second nature once you understand these three fundamental bet types. The key is matching the right bet type to your analysis and finding value in the odds offered.

Start by comparing lines across multiple sportsbooks to ensure you're getting the best number. Even a half-point difference in a spread or a few cents of juice can impact your long-term profitability. Track your bets by type to see where your strengths lie—you might discover you're better at totals than spreads, or that underdog moneylines fit your style.

Remember that sportsbooks set these lines to balance action and build in their edge, but that doesn't mean value doesn't exist. Sharp bettors identify when lines don't accurately reflect the true probability of outcomes and capitalize on those opportunities.
