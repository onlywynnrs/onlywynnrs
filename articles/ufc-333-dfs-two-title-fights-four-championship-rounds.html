UFC 333 DFS: Two Title Fights Break the Usual Rule

Every bout on this card prices to exactly $16,200 per pair, same as always. What is not the same is that UFC 333 has two title fights, and that single fact rewrites how you build.

Four championship-round seats instead of two

DraftKings cannot charge extra for championship rounds. Salary tracks the moneyline, and the moneyline says nothing about scheduled length. On a normal card that discount applies to two fighters. Here it applies to four.

Those four are the four highest projections on the slate in our simulation, and it is not close — the gap from the fourth of them to the fifth-best fighter on the card is roughly four points of mean score, and all four sit in the middle of the salary range rather than at the top.

The usual construction rule does not survive this. Tracked professional results say about seventy percent of optimal lineups contain exactly one five-round fighter. On this card our simulation produces seventy-two percent containing exactly two, nine percent containing three, and under one percent containing none. The rule is a function of there normally being one title fight. Change the input and the rule changes with it.

The pick'em at the top

Alexander Volkanovski is -112. Movsar Evloev is -108. That is as close to a coin flip as a main event gets, and the two of them cost $8,200 and $8,000.

Evloev comes in 20-0. He is a volume grappler with one of the lowest finish rates on the card, which in most formats would be a knock — but across twenty-five minutes, takedowns at five apiece plus control time at 1.8 a minute adds up faster than most knockouts do. He produces the highest mean score on the slate in our model and an eightieth-percentile outcome of 174.

Volkanovski is the higher-volume striker at 5.70 significant strikes a minute. Different path, similar destination.

Neither carries a favourite tax, because there is no favourite.

The Dvalishvili trap, and why we nearly walked into it

Our first model run had Merab Dvalishvili as the single best value on the board by a mile — 17.1 points per dollar, nearly double anything outside the title fights. The logic was obvious: career takedown rate of 5.97 per fifteen minutes, twenty-five minutes of fight, and a $7,400 salary that puts him twenty-third of twenty-eight.

Then we checked the rate against the right opponent.

Dvalishvili's career takedown accuracy is thirty-five percent. Against Petr Yan it is seventeen percent — thirteen landed from seventy-eight attempts across two fights. In their December rematch, which Dvalishvili lost by unanimous decision, he went two for twenty-nine.

That is not noise. That is a specific stylistic answer, demonstrated twice, to the exact thing his projection is built on. We replaced the career rate with the opponent-adjusted one and his mean fell from 126.6 to 109.3.

He is still the best points-per-dollar play on the slate at 14.8. He is no longer a freak, and the difference between those two numbers is the difference between a sound build and a lineup resting on a number that this matchup has already refuted.

The honest version of the Dvalishvili case: he scores 40.2 points from striking, 22.7 from takedowns and 12.5 from control in our average simulation, with a fourteen percent finish rate. He is a volume accumulator who needs the full twenty-five minutes, not a bonus play.

Where the rest of the value sits

With two championship-round seats eating $15,400 of the cap, the remaining four slots have $34,600 — about $8,650 each. That is comfortable, and it is why this slate supports a stronger build than most.

Grant Dawson at $8,500 is the highest-leverage piece in the mid-range: 4.10 takedown attempts per fifteen minutes against an opponent who will give up position, in a fight the market calls a coin flip at -108. He appears in thirty-two percent of our optimal lineups against twenty-two percent projected ownership.

Jacob Malkoun at $7,300 carries the highest leverage on the whole card at 1.58x. He is a +144 underdog with 3.40 takedown attempts per fifteen minutes — the archetype this scoring system pays and the field routinely ignores because he almost never finishes.

Iasmin Lucindo at $9,200 gets the one weight-miss edge available. Nicolle Caliari weighed 120.5 for a 115-pound bout, four and a half pounds over, against a fighter who was already a -300 favourite.

Abdul-Rakhman Yakhyaev at $9,400 is a -488 favourite with a high finish lean and the best floor on the slate at 83.3. Expensive, and the cap math works here in a way it usually does not.

$49,800. One fighter from each title fight, as the simulation says to build.

Worst case across all six field assumptions we tested: 1.74 percent to post a winning score, against roughly 0.0067 percent for a random entry. That is about 261x, and it is comfortably the strongest number we have published. The reason is structural rather than clever — when the four best plays on a card are all mid-priced, a lineup holding two of them starts a long way ahead.

What it needs: both title-fight picks winning happens about twenty-one percent of the time and lifts us 4.2x. If both lose, we are at zero, and that happens thirty percent of the time.

Ownership sums to 200 percent where a random six on a twenty-eight-fighter slate lands near 129, with under one expected duplicate. Chalky and we are fine with it — a split first prize beats a clean miss.

One flag. Our best regime this week is the one where the field arrives over-weighted to underdogs after last Saturday's results, which returns 2.05 percent. We are not assuming the field does that. We are noting that if it does, this build gains rather than suffers.
