UFC Edmonton DFS: Three Salaries That Never Caught Up to the Market

We went 3 for 6 last Saturday. Start there, because it is the only part of this that was tested.

What last week cost us

Our published lineup was Franco, Harris, Allen, Prado, Kamaka and Price. Here is how it graded.

Both of our most expensive favourites were finished inside the opening round by underdogs. Franco, who had stopped all ten of his pre-UFC opponents inside five minutes, got choked out in 109 seconds by a man who took the fight on short notice and missed weight by three and a half pounds.

Our thesis that week was that the card would finish and we were buying the finishes. The card did finish — eight of twelve ended inside the distance. We bought the wrong side of three of them.

The useful part is which side. Franco, Harris and Leon Shahbazyan were all priced on tape the UFC had never seen — regional records, Contender Series runs, pre-promotion finish rates. All three lost. Julius Walker, who was 1-3 in the UFC and had been knocked out in eight seconds in June, won. The market was not wrong about who was good. It was wrong about how confident to be, and it was wrong in the same direction every time.

That is the lesson we are carrying into this week, and this card is built to test it: seven of the twenty-six fighters here are making their UFC debut.

No gifts this week

Two of the structural edges we have used recently are simply absent.

All thirteen bouts price to exactly $16,200 per pair. There is no repriced fight, no late replacement, no stale pair total. And all twenty-six fighters made weight — a perfect Friday on the scale, which removes the single biggest edge from two weeks ago.

So the value has to come from somewhere narrower. It does, and it is hiding in plain sight.

Three salaries that never caught up

DraftKings sets MMA salaries off the moneyline and then leaves them alone. When a line moves hard after salaries are posted, the price goes stale. Usually that is one fight on a card. This week it is three, and in two of them the salary is pointing at the wrong fighter entirely.

Joel Faglier, $7,900. He is a -120 favourite. His opponent Javad Mahjoub is +100 and costs $8,300. DraftKings has the cheaper fighter as the favourite — the pair is priced backwards relative to the market. Faglier appears in thirty-two percent of our optimal lineups against roughly twenty-six percent projected ownership.

Steven Koslow, $8,000. This fight opened as a true coin flip at -110 both ways. Koslow has since been steamed to -150 while Chad Anheliger drifted to +125 — and Anheliger is the one priced at $8,200. Again, the favourite is the cheaper man.

SuYoung You, $7,700. Opened +190 against Cody Chovancek at -225. He is now +105 while Chovancek has softened to -125. That is a near pick'em in which one fighter costs $800 more than the other, and the $800 is on the wrong side.

None of these are reads on fighter quality. They are arithmetic. The market repriced three fights and the salary file did not.

The main event moved further than any of them

Joaquin Buckley opened at -300. He is now +105.

That is a 405-cent inversion in the headline fight, and there is no reported injury, no camp change, no weight issue behind it. The reporting attributes it to early money arriving fast plus Canadian home-crowd action on Mike Malott, who fights in Edmonton. The total sits at 3.5 rounds, meaning the market expects this to go long.

Unlike the three above, the salary file did catch this one — Malott is $8,400 and Buckley $7,800, correctly ordered. So there is no pricing edge here, just a genuinely close five-round fight where both men are top-five on this slate in points per dollar. DraftKings cannot charge extra for championship rounds, and these two are the only fighters getting twenty-five minutes.

We land on Malott, and the reason is the next section rather than anything about the fight itself.

The overcorrection

Here is the part that is a judgement call rather than arithmetic, and we will mark it as such.

Last Saturday was an underdog massacre. The field watched two top-priced favourites get starched in round one. The predictable consequence is that this week's entries arrive over-weighted to dogs — people fade chalk after chalk fails, which is backwards, because last week's result says nothing about this week's prices.

We did not assume that. We added it as a sixth ownership regime and tested the build against all six. If the field really does show up dog-drunk, our lineup's chance of posting a winning score goes up, from 0.98 percent in the worst regime to 1.18 percent — the best of the six. Correctly-priced favourites get cheaper in ownership terms precisely when everyone is reaching for underdogs.

Buckley is where this bites hardest. Under a dog-drunk field his projected ownership climbs to roughly fifty-two percent while Malott's falls to thirty-three. Same fight, same prices, and the leveraged side flips to the favourite.

Exactly $50,000. Three of the six are the stale-salary plays.

Blanchfield is the anchor — 3.3 takedown attempts per fifteen minutes is the highest grappling volume on the card, and in a scoring system paying five a takedown plus control time, that is a floor almost nobody else here has. Daukaus brings a high finish lean at a price the field is not paying. Malott is twenty-five minutes.

What the lineup needs: all three stale-salary plays to win happens about fourteen percent of the time and lifts us 6.4x. Malott winning is close to mandatory — our chance is 1.85x baseline if he does and essentially zero if he does not.

Worst-case across all six field assumptions: 0.98 percent, against roughly 0.0067 percent for a random entry. That is about 147x.

Ownership across the six sums to 198 percent where a random six-man roster on a twenty-six-fighter slate lands near 138. Chalkier than average, roughly one expected duplicate, and we are fine with that — a split first prize is a far better outcome than a clean miss.

One honest flag, given how last week went. Three of our six are in fights involving UFC debutants, and the single clearest lesson from Saturday is that the market prices unproven fighters with more confidence than it has earned. We have widened the variance on every debutant in the model rather than pretending we know how regional output translates. It is a wider cone, not a sharper one.
