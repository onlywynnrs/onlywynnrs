UFC Vegas 121: The Two Cheapest Fighters on the Board Are in 24% of Winning Lineups

Eleven fights became twelve on Thursday when Mickey Gall failed a pre-fight medical and the UFC booked Luis Hernandez against Sedriques Dumas on roughly two days' notice. That is the first thing to know about this slate, and it is not the most interesting one.

We ran forty thousand simulated versions of this card and fourteen thousand tournaments across all 42,832 legal rosters. The results point hard in one direction, and it is not where the money is going.

Two lines flipped completely this week

Rodolfo Vieira opened as a +177 underdog against Robert Bryczek. He is now a -155 favorite. That is not a nudge, it is a complete inversion, and those are worth stopping for.

The stated case against Vieira is real and specific: his takedown accuracy sits around 22 percent, his entries are telegraphed, and they degrade badly once he is tired or has been hit. He is a decorated grappler who has trouble getting the fight to the floor, which is the worst possible combination. Bryczek's path is to touch him up early and then simply refuse to go down.

So the market moved a full swing toward the fighter whose one path to victory is the thing he is worst at executing. Vieira is the highest-ceiling outcome on the card if he lands it — a submission generates enormous fantasy value — but the distribution is brutally bimodal.

Melissa Amaya opened at -115 and is now a +180 underdog against Valesca Machado in the strawweight TUF final. Another full flip in the opposite direction, in a fight between two fighters with one UFC appearance between them. Any projection either way on that bout is built on almost nothing, which the pricing does not reflect.

The cheap side of four fights is where the tournament is

Here is what the simulation produced, and it is unusually stark.

Christian Edwards appears in 24.1 percent of winning lineups. His projected ownership is 1.4 percent. That is the single biggest gap we have measured on any card this month.

Melissa Amaya: 21.0 percent of winners, 2.3 percent ownership.

Robert Bryczek: 27.2 percent of winners, 9.5 percent ownership.

Sedriques Dumas: 20.8 percent of winners, 4.7 percent ownership.

Raoni Barcelos: 34.4 percent of winners, 18.3 percent ownership.

Every one of those is the underdog side of a fight where the favorite is being bought hard. Edwards is the clearest: Bellato appears in 71 percent of optimizer-built lineups, the highest exposure on the entire slate, while the man across from him at $7,600 is in almost nobody's.

There is a mechanical reason. Edwards is priced as a live dog with a real finishing path, but he is making his second UFC appearance after a split-decision loss in his debut — no track record for a model to latch onto, no name for a casual entrant to recognise. He falls through both filters at once. That is the profile you want.

Why the top of the board is a trap here

Yazmin Jauregui is -1000, the biggest favorite on the card, and the most expensive fighter at $9,800. Optimizer builds give her zero exposure.

That is the salary cap talking. At $9,800 she consumes a fifth of your budget, and to fit her you must buy from a cheap tier where production collapses. She also has not fought in more than two years, and her last outing was a stunning loss as a heavy favorite. Her opponent has lost four straight, so the fight itself is likely a formality — but a formality against a fading opponent is precisely the kind of night that produces a decision win and eighty fantasy points, not a slate-breaking score.

Rinya Nakamura sits in similar territory: 1 percent optimizer exposure at $9,300, a year out of competition, against an opponent returning from two years away.

Neither is a bad fighter. Both are bad uses of a fifth of your cap.

The main event is the one piece of chalk worth paying for

Raul Rosas Jr. is the only fighter on this card getting five rounds, and he took Rob Font down sixteen times in his last outing.

Sixteen takedowns is eighty points before anything else happens. In a scoring system that pays five per takedown and accrues control time continuously, a grappler with that volume and twenty-five minutes to work is a different asset class from everyone else here. He posts the highest mean and by far the highest ceiling on the slate — a ninetieth percentile of 127 against 100 for the next-best fighter.

He will be roughly half-owned and he is still correct. Sometimes the chalk is chalk for a reason, and your edge has to come from the other five slots.

Barcelos is the interesting counterweight. He has won five straight, he is widely considered the most complete opponent Rosas has faced, and at $7,500 with the same twenty-five minutes available he shows up in 34 percent of winning lineups. If you want to fade the main-event favorite, this is a far better expression of it than fading the fight entirely.

The debutant nobody can price

Luis Hernandez is 8-0 with every win by knockout or submission, he earned his contract eleven days ago, and he is fighting on two days' notice as a short-notice replacement.

His opponent has lost three of his last four, submitted in each of the last two.

The fight is also generating attention for reasons that have nothing to do with fighting — Hernandez is a serving sheriff's deputy and his opponent has a documented arrest history, and that story has travelled well beyond MMA media this week. Narrative drives casual ownership more reliably than form does, which is why our model has him around 40 percent despite nobody having UFC data on him.

A finisher with no film, on two days' notice, against a man who keeps getting submitted, at 40 percent ownership. The upside is real and so is the crowd. That combination usually means pass, and our builds are split on him.

What we are doing

Rosas is the anchor. The ceiling gap is too large to engineer around and five rounds is free scoring time.

One premium fighter at most beyond him. The cap forces a punt for every expensive piece you add, and this slate's cheap tier is not where you want to be spending.

The differentiation comes from the underdog side of the Bellato, Machado, Vieira and Hernandez fights — Edwards, Amaya, Bryczek and Dumas. Four fighters, a combined ownership under 20 percent, and each one appears in more than a fifth of simulated winners.

And nobody gets rostered against their own opponent. Ever.
