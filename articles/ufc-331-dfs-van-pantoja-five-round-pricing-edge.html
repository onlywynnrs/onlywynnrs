UFC 331 DFS: DraftKings Is Charging Prelim Prices for Championship Rounds

Twelve fights at Crypto.com Arena on Saturday, headlined by Joshua Van defending the flyweight title against Alexandre Pantoja in a rematch neither man really got to have the first time.

One structural note before anything else. Renato Moicano vs. Brian Ortega 2 has been scrapped after Ortega withdrew with an injury, and Gable Steveson was elevated to the main card to fill the slot. If you pulled salaries early in the week, re-download them.

The clearest edge is the clock

DraftKings prices fighters on who is expected to win. It does not price them on how long they will be out there.

Four men on this card fight championship rounds. Arman Tsarukyan is $9,100, Joshua Van is $8,300, Alexandre Pantoja is $7,900 and Mauricio Ruffy is $7,100.

Now look at what a three-round prelim costs. JooSang Yoo is $9,000. Dooho Choi is $8,900. Charles Jourdain is $8,800. Iwo Baraniewski is $8,700.

The reigning flyweight champion, in a five-round main event, costs less than four fighters who will be locked in cages for fifteen minutes at most. Twenty-five minutes against fifteen is roughly forty percent more scoring time, and in a format where significant strikes and control time accrue by the second, that is not a rounding error. It is the largest structural advantage available on the slate and it is free.

Van is the one to focus on. He has won seven straight and is a narrow favorite at around -132, having opened as the underdog before the market moved to him. He is a volume striker in a division where fights routinely go the distance. That is the profile you want when you are buying minutes rather than finishes.

The three biggest numbers on the board come from the smallest samples

Sort this slate by fantasy points per fight and the top three are Iwo Baraniewski at 117.5, Ryan Gandra at 117.4 and Gable Steveson at 116.7.

Steveson has fought in the UFC exactly once. He knocked out Elisha Ellison in the first round of his debut in July, and that single performance is the entire average. He is 4-0 as a professional with all four wins ending inside the opening round, which is a genuinely impressive record and also four fights.

Gandra and Baraniewski are in the same territory — big per-fight numbers built on a handful of appearances.

None of that means they are bad plays. Steveson at -2000 against a heavyweight on a two-fight skid is about as close to a lock as this sport offers, and first-round finishes are worth enormous bonus points in this scoring system. The point is narrower: a 117 average built on two fights and a 103 average built on a dozen are not the same claim, and the slate's pricing treats them as though they are.

Van's 103.5 is a measured number. Tsarukyan's 93.9 is a measured number. Pantoja has thirty-six professional fights behind his 87.3. Those are the averages you can lean on.

Tsarukyan is the best pure DFS play on the card

He is unbeaten in five straight, most recently submitting Dan Hooker, and he has spent the layoff doing monthly grappling exhibitions. He is a -350 favorite in a five-round fight.

The scoring math loves exactly this. Takedowns score, control time scores by the second, and submission attempts score. A wrestler grinding a striker for twenty-five minutes generates points whether or not he finishes, which means his floor and his ceiling both sit high.

The question the fight turns on is Ruffy's takedown defense. He has been the division's most explosive striker this year, stopping Rafael Fiziev in February and battering Michael Chandler in June, and his hand speed is real. He has also failed to reach the third round in five of his last six fights, which cuts both ways — he either ends things early or things end early for him.

If you want the contrarian side of the co-main, Ruffy at $7,100 with five rounds available is the cheapest path to a big number on this card. Just understand you are betting on the knockout, not on a competitive twenty-five minutes.

Where the field will pile in

Steveson, Despaigne and Gandra are the gravity well. All three are heavy favorites with real finishing equity, and first-round finish bonuses are the single largest scoring event in this format. Expect all three to be heavily rostered, and expect a lot of lineups to look identical because of it.

Two of those are defensible. Robelis Despaigne at -700 faces Tai Tuivasa, who has lost seven consecutive UFC fights and is given roughly a thirteen percent chance of hearing the final bell. Gandra has seen five of his six fights end in the first round.

The third is where it gets expensive. Steveson at $9,900 eats a fifth of your cap for a fight that may last ninety seconds. A ninety-second finish is a wonderful outcome and also a capped one — there is only so much striking volume and control time available in ninety seconds. You are paying top price for a ceiling that the fight's own likely shape limits.

Fights to leave alone

JooSang Yoo vs. Michael Aswell Jr. Aswell has seen five of his six fights go the distance, and the market gives this one a strong chance of reaching the scorecards. Three rounds that end in a decision is the lowest-scoring outcome in this format.

Patricio Pitbull vs. Dooho Choi is compelling television and a poor DFS investment on the Pitbull side. He is 39, he is 1-2 since arriving in the UFC, and he is coming off a clear decision loss to Aaron Pico. Choi arrives on a three-fight winning streak with all three wins by TKO, and he is the correct side here at $8,900 — but at 29 percent implied, Pitbull is not the leverage play his price suggests.

Ozzy Diaz and Sean Sharaf are the punts everyone reaches for, and both are facing fighters who finish in the first round. Salary relief that ends in ninety seconds is not salary relief.

The construction

Anchor with minutes rather than with finishes. Tsarukyan and Van give you the two highest-floor pieces on the slate and they cost less than the prelim favorites everyone will roster.

Take one finish-equity favorite rather than three. Despaigne at $9,600 is the best of them on matchup, and skipping Steveson is the cheapest differentiation available on a card where he will be on most rosters.

Use the coinflips for salary. Shahbazyan at -156 and Ferreira at +133 is a genuine toss-up, and the ten-second rule applies — when the market cannot separate two fighters, take the one who scores more per dollar.

And keep both sides of a fight out of the same lineup. Always.
