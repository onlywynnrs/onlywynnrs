UFC Baku Fight-by-Fight DFS Breakdown: Fiziev vs Torres Full Card

UFC Baku: The Finish-Hunter's Fight-by-Fight Guide

The UFC's second trip to Azerbaijan is a Caucasus showcase — nearly every fighter on the card carries an "-ov" or "-ev," and the region's reputation for producing aggressive, high-finish-rate maulers shows up all over the board. For DFS, that's the headline: this is a finish-heavy slate where the DraftKings scoring (first-round finish +90, decision +30) rewards roster spots that can end the night early. Here's the honest fight-by-fight read — styles, camps, recent form, and the DFS angle on each.

We don't sell certainty. Fights are chaos, and anyone telling you they "know" these outcomes is guessing with confidence. What follows is how we read the matchups and where the value and leverage sit.

Main Event — Rafael Fiziev vs. Manuel Torres (Lightweight, 5 rounds)

A genuine stylistic puzzle, priced as a coin-flip. Fiziev, the Azerbaijani-born striker out of Tiger Muay Thai, is one of the most technical kickboxers in the division — four Fight of the Night awards, two of them against Justin Gaethje. The problem is form: he's 1-4 over his last five and is coming off a February knockout loss to Mauricio Ruffy. Torres is the inverse — a Mexican knockout artist whose finishing résumé is genuinely rare: 16 of 17 career wins inside the distance, and all seven of his UFC fights have ended in the first round.

The fight comes down to one question: can Torres' early pressure crack a fading-but-elite technician before Fiziev's range and timing take over? The DFS wrinkle is the five-round format. Torres has never seen the second round in the UFC, so if Fiziev survives the storm and drags it long, Torres' fantasy floor collapses. DFS angle: Torres is the mispriced ceiling of the slate — mid-pack salary, tournament-winning upside if he lands early, near-zero if it goes long. A pure GPP swing, not a safe play. Fiziev is the safer scorer only if he finishes, which his recent form doesn't guarantee.

Co-Main — Shara Magomedov vs. Michel Pereira (Middleweight)

The "Bullet" is a volume striker built on low kicks and body knees that set up sustained bursts, and he's a heavy favorite here. The honest caveat: he hasn't been overly impressive in his two fights since his lone loss to Michael Page. Pereira is the wild card of the night — an acrobatic, unpredictable Brazilian who literally re-signed with the UFC specifically to take this fight. He's 1-3 in his last four and needs not just a win but a statement.

DFS angle: Magomedov is a clean chalk anchor with finishing pop. Pereira is the headline leverage dog — his style means he's never out of a fight, and at a cheap salary he's the kind of low-owned finisher who lets you afford two premium anchors. If Pereira lands one of his trademark wild sequences, lineups that have him take the top.

Ikram Aliskerov vs. Brunno Ferreira (Middleweight)

One of the cleaner "finish is coming" spots on the card. Aliskerov is a Dagestani combat sambo specialist — heavy hands paired with offensive wrestling, a southpaw with a high work rate who's knocking on the top 10. Ferreira is an explosive finisher in his own right but dropped his last bout, and his own camp is publicly betting on his unpredictability to neutralize Aliskerov's grappling. The betting market expects this one to end early (the under 1.5 rounds is a popular play).

DFS angle: Aliskerov is a strong cash-game and GPP option — a favorite who finishes is exactly what this scoring rewards. Ferreira is a live-dog dart with real one-punch equity, but the wrestling disadvantage caps his floor.

Nazim Sadykhov vs. Matheus Camilo (Lightweight)

Sadykhov is one of the hometown stars — he already has Baku history, having finished Nikolas Motta with a right hook in the second round on the UFC's first Azerbaijan visit, in front of this same crowd. Camilo is a tougher-than-his-record grappler coming off a unanimous-decision win at Madison Square Garden, making his first walk of 2026.

DFS angle: Sadykhov is a mid-priced favorite with real finishing ability and the crowd behind him — a solid value piece. Camilo's path is control and decision, which is a low-ceiling profile in this scoring; fade him in GPP unless you want pure contrarian exposure.

Asu Almabayev vs. Charles Johnson (Flyweight)

A ranked flyweight bout. Almabayev has built a 6-1 UFC record, his only loss coming via an uncalled eye poke against Manel Kape, and he's trending up on a two-fight streak capped by a submission of Alex Perez. Johnson is dangerous — he just landed a statement knockout over Lone'er Kavanagh.

DFS angle: Almabayev profiles as a high-floor favorite who scores through volume and grappling, useful in cash. Johnson carries the slate's classic leverage shape — a live underdog with legitimate knockout power who'll go underowned.

Abus Magomedov vs. Michal Oleksiejczuk (Middleweight — main card opener)

The single most important fight to understand for DFS, because of one factor: cardio. Abus Magomedov is a 35-year-old veteran with excellent kicks, real power, and underrated grappling — but an infamous gas tank that causes him to decline severely midway through the second round. Oleksiejczuk, a former light heavyweight now training at Fighting Nerds, has looked sharper since the move and brings power; the underdog money has been coming in, with analysts expecting a second- or third-round stoppage once Magomedov fades.

DFS angle: This is a leverage spot. Magomedov can win — and score big — if he finishes early, but his fade pattern makes him a risky anchor. Oleksiejczuk is a live dog whose path (late stoppage) is exactly the high-value finish this scoring rewards. One of the better contrarian finishers on the board.

The Prospect Showcases — Yakhyaev, Donchenko, Reyes

The undercard is loaded with heavily favored regional prospects, and a few carry big DraftKings scoring averages. Abdul Rakhman Yakhyaev and Daniil Donchenko are local talents favored hard against overmatched opposition — premium-salary anchors with finishing upside, the kind of chalk that can actually post a tournament score rather than grind a decision. Javier Reyes carries the highest scoring average on the slate and has openly set a top-15 goal for 2026; he projects as a finisher against the wrestling-reliant Kaan Ofli, though Ofli's grappling is the contrarian path if you think Reyes is priced too short.

DFS angle: These are your salary-relief anchors and ceiling plays. The risk with hyped prospects is that a dominant favorite can still grind a slow finish or decision; pair them, don't stack the whole card on them.

The Thin-Data Fights — Honest Caveats

Several bouts feature fighters with little or no UFC scoring history, and we won't pretend to have reads we don't. Farman Hasanov (an Azerbaijani debutant), Tahir Abdullayev, Jefferson Nascimento, and Theodor Berggren are genuine unknowns in this scoring system. Jean Matsumoto is the most established of the bottom group — a fringe top-15 bantamweight whose last three fights all went to split decision, which is a real flag: a decision-heavy fighter is a low-ceiling DFS profile even when he wins. Nursulton Ruziboev and Bekzat Almakhan round out the board with finishing tools but limited data.

DFS angle: Unknowns cut both ways. In a large-field tournament, an undervalued debutant finisher is a leverage dart almost nobody else will click. In cash games, the lack of a track record is a reason to stay away. Know which game you're playing before you roster them.

The Bottom Line for Your Lineup

This is a card built for finish-hunting. The framework: anchor with one or two premium finishers (Yakhyaev, Donchenko, or Shara Magomedov), find value at the bottom with live dogs who can end a fight (Pereira, Oleksiejczuk, Johnson), and take your tournament swing on the mispriced ceiling — Torres, whose first-round-or-bust profile is the highest-variance, highest-upside spot on the slate. Every roster spot should have a realistic path to a finish, because decision-only fighters are salary you can't afford to waste.
