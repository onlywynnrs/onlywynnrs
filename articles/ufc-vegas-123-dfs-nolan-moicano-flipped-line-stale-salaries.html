UFC Vegas 123 DFS Picks: The Main Event Flipped and Nobody Moved the Salaries

Twelve bouts went up for Saturday's card at the Apex, and the first thing worth doing with any DraftKings MMA slate is adding up each fight's two salaries. The reason is that DraftKings prices MMA almost purely off the moneyline, and every bout's pair sums to exactly $16,200. When a pair sums to something else, it means the bout was repriced after a replacement, and that gap is the single most reliable edge available.

Every one of the twelve pairs this week sums to exactly $16,200.

So there is no repricing edge on this card. No orphaned fighter DraftKings forgot to remove, no bout quietly marked down after an opponent change. Each salary is a clean translation of the line that existed when the board went up. Which means the entire edge has to come from somewhere else: what the market did to those lines afterward.

It did a lot.

The main event flipped and the salary did not follow

Tom Nolan opened at -170 over Renato Moicano. He is now a +102 underdog. That is a thirteen-point swing in implied win probability, and it happened because the bout exists at all as a replacement booking — Brian Ortega withdrew injured in mid-September, Nolan was slotted in within a day, and the opening number was priced on a 26-year-old on a five-fight streak rather than on the matchup.

DraftKings still has Nolan as the nominal favorite, at $8,200 against Moicano's $8,000. The market has Moicano at roughly 53%.

So the cheaper fighter in the main event is also the market favorite. That is the kind of two-line arithmetic edge that needs no read on either man, and there is a second one further down the card: Cody Durden at $7,900 is a slight favorite over Ode Osbourne at $8,300. Cheaper and favored, again.

Four other bouts moved hard toward the favorite after salaries locked — Jean-Paul Lebosnoyani from -175 to about -308, Joe Kropschot from -215 to -328, Lucia Szabova from -400 to -738, Piera Rodriguez from -195 to -292. One moved hard the other way: JeongYeong Lee opened -300 and is now -167, which is the market pricing a seventeen-month layoff that the salary never accounted for.

A thing we had wrong, and then measured

The obvious conclusion from a cheap main-event fighter is "buy the twenty-five minutes." Five-round fights are the only place on a card where a fighter can accumulate for 25 minutes instead of 15, and the cheaper of the two five-round fighters is usually where the slate's leverage lives.

Except Moicano's fights do not go twenty-five minutes. In twenty UFC appearances, exactly one has ever reached the fourth round, and he lost it. His average fight time is 9:24. Four of his five career losses by stoppage came in the first two rounds. He is a fast starter who is also most vulnerable early, and at 37 he has essentially no championship-round record to buy.

Nolan is the one whose fights actually go long. Every one of his five KO/TKO wins came before the UFC or in his debut; at this level he has gone the full fifteen in three of his last four, with zero knockdowns in six UFC fights. He throws 4.84 significant strikes a minute.

That sent us to check something directly rather than assume it. A first-round win pays a 90-point bonus. A decision pays 30. That 60-point gap is enormous, and the instinct is that finishing always wins. So we measured, for every fighter on the card, their mean score when they win inside the first round against their mean score when they win a decision.

For 21 of the 24 fighters, the first-round finish scores more. The 60-point head start is not something fifteen minutes of ordinary output can close.

Worth saying plainly, because we have overstated this category before: control time is a small bucket. Across all 24 fighters, the most anyone averages from control is 4.3 points. It is a tiebreak, not a thesis. The significant-strike doubling and the takedowns are what move scores.

The number that wasn't what it said

Piera Rodriguez is the third-most-expensive fighter on the board at $9,200, and her listed takedown rate is 4.04 per fifteen minutes. That would make her one of the best plays on the slate.

It is takedowns attempted. She has three landed across roughly 94 minutes of UFC cage time — a real rate near 0.48 per fifteen minutes, less than an eighth of the published figure. Her last five UFC wins are all decisions and she has never recorded a submission win in her career.

Corrected, she finishes 13th on the board in points per dollar and appears in the optimal lineup 3.6% of the time — against modelled ownership near 22%. That is the worst ratio of the twenty-four, at the third-highest price.

A related trap sits at the very top. Szabova is the biggest favorite on the card at 83.6%, and the most expensive fighter at $9,600 — and she lands in the optimal lineup only 20.9% of the time. She wins and she does not score. She is also making her UFC debut, as is Kropschot at $9,300: the two priciest fighters on the board have zero UFC fights between them, priced entirely on regional tape. Five of the twenty-four have two or fewer UFC appearances.

Smith is the engine and he is also the chalk. At 10.82 points per $1,000 he is the best play on the board by a 19% margin over second place, and that survives every stress test we ran: cutting his finish rate, cutting his takedown rate, and adding fatigue all leave him ranked first. His projection rests on a four-fight UFC sample, which normally disqualifies a rate from being trusted — the reason we trust the direction here is an independent credential rather than the sample itself, an NCAA All-American wrestling background. He fought 28 days ago on short notice, which is the real risk.

What this build is, and what it isn't

It is a bet on the main event, and we would rather say so than dress it up.

Against a field modelled at 42.5% on Moicano, this lineup returns roughly 1.3x the chalk build's expected value across the full payout ladder. But almost all of that comes from one branch: when Moicano loses, it outperforms chalk by a factor of roughly 98. When Moicano wins, it is close to worthless.

It also has a lower outright win rate than the chalk build — 0.265% against 0.337% — and its median finish lands just outside the cash line. More than half the time this ticket does nothing. That is the intended shape for a single entry into a 17,000-person field paying a third of its prize pool to one person: we are giving up about five points of win probability to gain fourteen points of ownership relief on a near-coinflip the field has picked a side of.

What the model still gets wrong

Four fighters here have exactly one UFC fight and two have none, so six of twenty-four are running on widened guesses rather than data. The largest single unverified input is Farman Hasanov's takedown volume: he reportedly broke a welterweight single-round takedown record in his debut, but no source attaches an actual count to that claim, so his rate is shrunk hard toward the division mean and flagged.

The model also still cannot produce main-event stacks at the right frequency. Tracked results say the optimal lineup holds both five-round fighters about 5% of the time; ours does it 0.6%, because it finds a six-winner optimal 93.6% of the time and therefore rarely generates the scenario that makes stacking correct. On this card the gap is wide enough that it does not change the decision — stacked builds graded roughly six times worse — but it is a known hole, not a solved one.

And Julian Erosa has said he intends to retire after Saturday. He is the highest-variance fighter on the board by a distance: 34 of his 44 professional fights ended inside the distance in one direction or the other, with six of his eight knockout losses coming in the first round. A declared retirement fight is a motivational variable with no parameter in any model. We flagged it rather than pretending to price it.
