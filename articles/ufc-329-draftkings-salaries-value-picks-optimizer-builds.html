UFC 329 DraftKings Salaries: Best Value Picks & Optimizer Builds

The Salaries Are Out — Here's Where the Value Actually Is

DraftKings released UFC 329 pricing, and the salary structure tells a story of its own. You get $50,000 for six fighters on a card where the most expensive fighter costs $9,900 and the main event favorite is somehow only sixth-priciest. When pricing and probability disagree, that gap is your edge. Here's the tier-by-tier read.

The Pricing Anomaly of the Slate: Holloway at $9,000

Max Holloway is a roughly 68% favorite in a five-round main event — and he's priced below Alessandro Costa and Luke Riley. Let that sink in. Five-round fights are worth more fantasy minutes, Holloway is one of the highest-volume strikers in UFC history (86.2 career FPPF, elite for a decision-capable fighter), and the finish threat against a returning McGregor is real. At $9,000, he's the best pure value on the board relative to role, format, and probability. The field will still under-own him relative to McGregor's star pull, which makes the correct side also the leverage side — a rare gift.

McGregor at $7,200 with an 88.2 career FPPF is the trap dressed as a bargain. The scoring average is from his prime; the price is tempting; the ownership will be enormous. If he turns back the clock, half the field has him. That's negative leverage on a 35% underdog.

The $9,000+ Tier: Pick One, Maybe Two

Gable Steveson ($9,900) is the most expensive fighter with zero UFC data — you're paying top dollar purely for the -2800 line and the expectation of a fast wrestling finish. He'll be the highest-owned fighter on the slate. Farid Basharat ($9,600) is 15-0 against a short-notice debutant — an 86% favorite whose grappling racks up takedown points even without the knockout. Adrian Yanez ($9,400) is the cleanest finish-hunting buy up top: a boxer at -410 against Garbrandt's compromised chin, and his 68.6 FPPF undersells the matchup. Luke Riley ($9,300) at 13-0 with a 79.9 FPPF is priced for the hype.

The math problem: you cannot roster all four. Two of these plus Holloway eats $27,000+ of your $50,000, forcing punts elsewhere. Tournament separation comes from which premium you skip — and the boldest, most defensible skip is Steveson, the priciest fighter with the least data and the most ownership.

The Mid-Tier Gold: $8,500–$8,900

This is where the slate's real firepower hides.

Damian Pinas ($8,900) carries a 118.9 FPPF — the second-highest scoring rate on the entire card — at a price $1,000 below Steveson. A 69% favorite who scores like that is a build-around, not a fill-in. Ryan Gandra ($8,600) is even more extreme: 130.6 FPPF, the highest on the slate, priced in the middle tier as a modest -135 favorite. Early-card fighters with elite scoring rates and medium prices are exactly how winning rosters get built while everyone stares at the main event. Benoit Saint Denis ($8,500) rounds out the trio — 101.0 FPPF, four straight finishes, in the fight the market has flagged for violence.

If there's one structural take in this article, it's this: the Pinas–Gandra–BSD mid-tier is the engine room of this slate. All three project as finishers, all three cost less than the name-brand chalk, and rostering two of them funds a Holloway anchor comfortably.

The Underdog Values: $7,400–$7,900

Mario Bautista ($7,900) — a 79.4 FPPF underdog whose wrestling pace generates points even in a decision. Against Sandhagen's decision-heavy style, he's the rare dog with a floor. Paddy Pimblett ($7,700) at 93.0 FPPF in the guaranteed-violence co-main is the ceiling dart of the tier. Nikita Krylov ($7,500) almost never sees a scorecard — all-finish profiles at plus money are pure GPP fuel. Brandon Royval ($7,400) scores in chaos even when losing, but losses still cap him.

The Punt Range: Handle With Care

Kai Kamaka III ($6,900) and Cody Garbrandt ($6,800) are the salary-relief options with a pulse — Garbrandt's counter power gives him genuine one-punch equity against Yanez, and at massive underdog ownership he's the punt that wins a tournament if it lands. John Garza ($6,600) and Elisha Ellison ($6,300) are near-zero equity plays; Ellison's 1.6 FPPF is the market telling you plainly not to do it. Punting one spot to afford three premiums is a real strategy. Punting two is a prayer.

Three Builds That Make Sense

The Balanced Anchor: Holloway ($9,000) + Pinas ($8,900) + Gandra ($8,600) + BSD ($8,500) + Bautista ($7,900) + Kamaka ($6,900) = $49,800. Four high-FPPF finishers, a floor dog, one punt — and zero Steveson exposure, which is the leverage.

The Chalk-Eater: Steveson ($9,900) + Yanez ($9,400) + Holloway ($9,000) + Gandra ($8,600) + Krylov ($7,500) + Garbrandt... doesn't fit. That's the point — the chalk build forces you down to Garza/Ellison territory. Eating all the chalk means eating a dead roster spot. Choose your favorites like they cost something, because they do.

The Full Leverage: Basharat ($9,600) + Pinas ($8,900) + BSD ($8,500) + McKinney ($8,400) + Pimblett ($7,700) + Kamaka ($6,900) = $50,000 on the nose. No main event exposure at all — if Holloway/McGregor produces a dud decision while the undercard produces violence, this roster laps the field.

We don't sell certainty, and we won't pretend salary math is destiny — fights are chaos. But pricing inefficiencies are real, and this card has three big ones: Holloway priced under the hype tier, the Pinas/Gandra mid-tier scoring engines, and a punt range with exactly one live option. Build accordingly.
