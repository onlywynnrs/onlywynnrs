How to Read Betting Lines: Spreads, Totals, and Moneylines Explained

Understanding the Three Main Types of Betting Lines

If you're new to sports betting in 2026, walking up to a sportsbook board or opening a betting app can feel overwhelming. Those numbers, plus and minus signs, and different betting options might look like a foreign language at first. But once you understand the three core bet types—point spreads, totals, and moneylines—you'll be reading betting lines like a pro.

Every bet you place falls into one of these three categories, and each serves a different purpose depending on what you think will happen in a game. Let's break down exactly how to read and interpret each type so you can make informed betting decisions.

Point Spreads: Leveling the Playing Field

The point spread is designed to create equal betting action on both sides of a matchup by giving the underdog a head start and the favorite a deficit to overcome. When you see a line like Team A -7.5 vs. Team B +7.5, that number represents how many points the favorite needs to win by.

If you bet on the favorite at -7.5, they must win by 8 or more points for your bet to cash. If you take the underdog at +7.5, they can lose by 7 or fewer points, or win outright, and you still win your bet.

The number next to the spread—usually -110 or -115—is the juice or vig, which represents how much you need to risk to win $100. At -110, you'd bet $110 to win $100. This is how sportsbooks make their profit regardless of the game outcome.

Point spreads are most common in football and basketball, where scoring systems make point differentials meaningful. The key to spread betting is determining whether the favorite will dominate enough to cover that number, or if the underdog will keep it closer than expected.

Totals (Over/Under): Predicting Combined Scoring

Totals betting, also called over/under betting, has nothing to do with who wins. Instead, you're wagering on whether the combined score of both teams will be over or under a number set by the sportsbook.

For example, if a total is set at 215.5 points, you can bet the over if you think both teams combined will score 216 or more, or the under if you believe they'll score 215 or fewer. That half-point (.5) is crucial—it eliminates the possibility of a push, where your bet would be refunded if the total landed exactly on the number.

Totals betting requires you to analyze offensive firepower, defensive efficiency, pace of play, and game conditions. A fast-paced game between two high-scoring teams might warrant an over bet, while a defensive struggle between methodical teams could be an under opportunity.

Like spreads, totals usually carry -110 odds on both sides, meaning you're risking $110 to win $100 whether you choose over or under. Shopping for the best total across different sportsbooks can make a significant difference—finding 214.5 instead of 215.5 could be the difference between winning and losing your bet.

Moneylines: Straight-Up Winners

The moneyline is the simplest bet to understand: pick who wins the game, period. No spreads, no totals—just pick the winner. However, the odds reflect each team's probability of winning, which affects your potential payout.

Favorites are marked with a minus sign (like -180), meaning you need to risk that amount to win $100. Underdogs carry a plus sign (like +160), meaning a $100 bet would profit that amount if they win.

The bigger the favorite, the more you need to risk for a smaller return. A -300 favorite requires a $300 bet to win $100—a significant risk even if that team is heavily favored. Meanwhile, a +250 underdog returns $250 profit on a $100 bet, but that higher payout reflects their lower probability of winning.

Moneyline betting shines when you have strong conviction about an outcome but don't want to worry about margins. It's popular in baseball and hockey, where 1-0 or 2-1 games make spreads less meaningful, though it's available for every sport.

Putting It All Together: Reading a Full Betting Board

When you look at a complete betting board, you'll see all three bet types listed for each game. A typical line might display: Team A -7.5 (-110) / +160 / O 215.5 (-110) and Team B +7.5 (-110) / -180 / U 215.5 (-110).

This tells you everything you need to know: Team A is a 7.5-point favorite with a +160 moneyline, Team B is a 7.5-point underdog with a -180 moneyline, and the total is 215.5 points. Understanding how to quickly parse this information helps you identify value and make smarter betting decisions.

The best bettors don't just pick winners—they find situations where the odds offer value compared to the actual probability of an outcome. Sometimes the spread offers better value than the moneyline, or the total presents a clearer opportunity than picking a side.
