UFC 332 DFS Picks: The $16,200 Rule and the One Bout DraftKings Repriced

DraftKings priced all fourteen bouts on this card so that each pair of fighters sums to exactly $16,200. Thirteen of them, anyway. One sums to $15,200, and that is where the slate opens up.

Start with what the symmetry does to lineup construction, because it governs everything else.

The $16,200 rule

Six roster spots, a $50,000 cap. That is $8,333 a slot. Half of $16,200 is $8,100. A roster of six fighters priced at the exact midpoint of their bouts costs $48,600 and leaves $1,400 of slack.

Every dollar above $8,100 you spend on a favorite has to come back out of someone priced below $8,100. On most slates you hunt for the favorite whose price has not caught up to his odds. Here there is no such thing, because the pricing is mechanical. Payton Talbott at -650 costs $9,600 and his opponent costs $6,600. Damian Pinas at -500 costs $9,400 and his opponent costs $6,800. The tax is uniform, which makes the one exception loud.

The bout that got repriced

Benardo Sopaj withdrew injured. Anthony Romero came in on three days' notice and the fight moved up to featherweight. Marcus McGhee was around -150 against Sopaj. He is roughly -500 against Romero.

DraftKings repriced the pair to $15,200 — McGhee $8,500, Romero $6,700. Every other favorite at that number costs $9,300 or more, which makes McGhee the cheapest -500 shot on the board by about $800. In a six-slot format $800 is most of a tier.

Two things come with him. He does not finish much, and the market has his fight going past two and a half rounds — in a format paying 90 points for a first-round stoppage and 30 for a decision, that is a real ceiling cap. And this card is at roughly 4,300 feet in Salt Lake City, which turns late rounds into a grind.

He is going to be popular. He is also correct. Those are not in conflict, and this is the part people get wrong about chalk: a cheap fighter at -500 is not a trap just because everyone sees it.

The cheapest twenty-five minutes on the board

Natalia Silva and Wang Cong are the only two fighters here who get five rounds, and they cost $8,700 and $7,500.

That is unusual. Fantasy scoring is a volume game, and championship-distance fighters have sixty-seven percent more time to accumulate, so they normally carry a premium. Silva arrives on a fourteen-fight run with wins over three former champions, absorbing only 2.36 significant strikes a minute and defending sixty-six percent of what comes at her. Cong is 10-1, two inches taller with a reach edge, and genuinely powerful.

The catch on Silva is that her likeliest path is a tactical decision — the market has this going the distance around three-quarters of the time, with Silva by decision the single most likely outcome. A five-round decision from a counter-striker is a good score, not a slate-breaking one. Her simulated ceiling lands below Ateba Gautier's, and Gautier costs $100 more for three rounds.

Cong is the better side for a tournament. At $7,500 you are buying twenty-five minutes from a pressure fighter, and underdogs who get championship rounds have the widest outcome distribution on any card. The risk is cardio — she stepped in after Valentina Shevchenko withdrew with a back and shoulder ligament injury and the title was vacated, and altitude punishes exactly that. That risk is why she is cheap.

Why the top of the board is affordable this week

Normally $9,800 is unusable in a structure like this, because spending it forces two punts and the cheapest fighters on a card are cheap for a reason — they are being fed to finishers.

Not here. Khaos Williams at $7,000 and Wang Cong at $7,500 are both live. Williams has never been knocked out and has five knockouts in seven UFC wins; he faces Roberto Soldic, a heavy favorite making his UFC debut having fought once since the middle of 2023, carrying a layoff and a new weight cut into altitude. That is a lot of unpriced uncertainty on the favorite's side of a fight where the underdog costs $7,000.

Which means the two cheap slots are not sacrifices, and that changes the math at the top. Jacobe Smith is 12-0 with the best first-round finish odds on the card and the best projection on the slate by a clear margin. Our own simulation kept building him into the strongest rosters, and the reason is simply that the punts required to afford him are good punts this week rather than dead ones.

Worth being precise about this, because the general rule points the other way. Avoiding the most expensive fighter in a tightly symmetric cap structure is usually correct. It is wrong here, and the thing that makes it wrong has nothing to do with Smith — it is who happens to be priced at the bottom of this particular card.

The leverage name

We ran sixty thousand simulated versions of the card, then evaluated rosters against five different assumptions about how the field builds — optimizer-heavy, casual-heavy, favorite-chasing, name-chasing, and balanced.

One fighter survives all five, and not narrowly. Ateba Gautier appears in the strongest rosters under every regime at roughly double the rate of the next name.

The reason is archetype arbitrage. The field is paying $9,300 to $9,800 for the obvious finishers. Gautier is the same archetype at $8,800, against Roman Kopylov — who is thirty-five, has been finished only twice in twenty fights, and has absorbed a lot of damage getting there. A durable opponent caps the ceiling slightly, but durability at thirty-five is a depreciating asset.

Two things against Gautier, stated plainly. His one previous UFC appearance that did not end in a knockout was a decision in which he was outstruck 59-34 and won on knockdowns — a thin, lumpy resume, and worth noting that the man who outstruck him is a +383 underdog on this same card. And his projection leans on power rather than volume, which is the highest-variance input there is.

Johnny Walker is the other name that holds across all five. He is making his heavyweight debut against a ranked opponent, which suppresses interest and widens outcomes at the same time. Heavyweight shortens fights, and shorter fights mean bigger finish bonuses.

What we will not pretend

Ranking individual six-man rosters by how often they win a simulated tournament does not work. A single roster wins a twenty-thousand-entry field a handful of times out of a few thousand trials, and the gap between the best and the tenth-best is entirely inside the noise. We tried it, got numbers that bounced between zero and one tenth of a percent, and threw them out.

What does work is scoring every roster against a modelled winning bar — in this case about 529 points, which came out nearly identical under all five field assumptions — across all sixty thousand simulations. That gives a stable comparison instead of a lucky one.

We also do not have real ownership before lock. Nobody does. The ownership figures on our slate page are modelled and labelled as modelled, and the whole point of testing five regimes is that we do not trust any single one of them.

The lineup

Jacobe Smith, Marcus McGhee, Ateba Gautier, Johnny Walker, Wang Cong, Khaos Williams. Exactly $50,000.

Gautier is the edge. Smith is the projection. McGhee is the repriced discount. Walker and Cong are the variance, and Williams is the fighter whose opponent has the most unpriced risk on the card.

One honest caveat: this is not a contrarian lineup. Our modelled ownership for these six sums to about 191 percent, where an average six-man roster would land near 129. Three of these fighters are popular, and they are popular for correct reasons. The differentiation here comes from Gautier being underpriced for his archetype and from pairing the slate's most expensive fighter with two cheap fighters who can actually score — not from avoiding anyone the field likes.
