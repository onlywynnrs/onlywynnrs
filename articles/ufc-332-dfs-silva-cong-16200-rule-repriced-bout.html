UFC 332 DFS Picks: Control Time, the Five-Round Discount, and One Repriced Bout

DraftKings prices MMA fighters almost entirely off the moneyline. That is not a secret — it is how the format works, and it means every bout on a card prices out to the same pair total. On this card that number is $16,200, and thirteen of the fourteen bouts hit it exactly.

One does not. That one is where the slate opens up. But it is not the biggest edge here, so start with the thing that is.

The five-round discount

Natalia Silva and Wang Cong are the only two fighters on this card who get twenty-five minutes. They cost $8,700 and $7,500.

DraftKings does not charge extra for championship rounds. It cannot — salary tracks the moneyline, and the moneyline says nothing about scheduled length. So the two fighters with sixty-seven percent more scoring time than everybody else are priced as though they have the same fifteen minutes as a prelim fighter.

Here is why that matters more than it looks. DraftKings pays 0.4 points for a significant strike, 0.2 for every other strike, and 0.03 per second of control time — which is 1.8 points a minute for doing nothing but holding position. Those are all per-minute categories. Ten extra minutes of a fighter landing four and a half significant strikes a minute is roughly eighteen points of striking alone, before control, before takedowns, before the win bonus.

Silva is the single most frequent member of the optimal lineup in our simulations — she appears in forty-two percent of them, well clear of anyone else, including every expensive favorite on the card. She absorbs only 2.36 significant strikes a minute, defends sixty-six percent of what comes at her, and arrives on a fourteen-fight run with wins over three former champions. Her likeliest outcome is a five-round decision, and in this scoring system a five-round decision from a high-volume counter-striker is not a consolation prize. It is a top-five score.

A note on honesty here: an earlier version of this article argued the opposite. It said Silva's ceiling sat below the mid-priced favorites and that Cong was the better side of the fight. That conclusion came out of a model that had significant strikes at half their real value and no control-time term at all. Fixing the scoring moved her projected ceiling from the middle of the board to second overall. The error was ours, it mattered, and it is the kind of thing that only surfaces if you check your scoring engine against the actual rules rather than against last week's output.

The bout that got repriced

Benardo Sopaj withdrew injured. Anthony Romero came in on three days' notice and the fight moved up to featherweight. Marcus McGhee was around -150 against Sopaj. He is roughly -500 against Romero.

This is the single most reliable edge in MMA DFS: salaries are set early and do not move, so when a fighter's opponent changes and their win probability jumps, the price is stale by definition. DraftKings did partially adjust — it repriced the pair to $15,200, McGhee $8,500 and Romero $6,700, against $16,200 everywhere else — but a -500 favorite at $8,500 is still the cheapest shot at that tier on the board by about $800.

The catch is that McGhee does not finish. His fight is favored to go past two and a half rounds, and in a format paying 90 points for a first-round stoppage and 30 for a decision, that is a real ceiling cap. He is also going to be the most-rostered fighter on this slate. Our model has him in thirty-three percent of optimal lineups against roughly forty-seven percent projected ownership — the only piece on the card where the field is clearly ahead of the value.

He is correct and he is over-owned at the same time. In a tournament, that combination is a fade.

Control time is where the quiet points are

Control time replaced positional advances in this scoring system, and it changes who is valuable in a way that moneylines do not capture.

A takedown is worth 5 points. Control time is worth 1.8 a minute, so just under three minutes of control equals one takedown. That creates a genuine tension: the fighter who takes you down and holds you there can only score one takedown per round, because you have to stand up before he can do it again. The fighter who takes you down, lets you up, and takes you down again scores five points every time.

So the best takedown scorers are not the best wrestlers. They are the wrestlers good enough to land takedowns against someone who keeps getting back up. That is usually a lighter-weight fighter, because cardio allows repeated attempts and lower bodyweight makes holding position harder.

Alden Coria at $7,600 is that archetype on this card. Six takedowns across three UFC wins, a slick boxer with real volume, and a bout our model expects to go the full fifteen minutes against a more explosive opponent. He is a bantamweight who cycles position rather than smothering it.

Mick Parkin is the mirror image and the highest-leverage fighter on the board by our numbers — twenty-five percent of optimal lineups against roughly fifteen percent ownership. Heavyweights hold people down, which is bad for takedown counts and excellent for control time, and at $7,800 as a live underdog nobody is accounting for the 1.8 points a minute he can bank while doing very little.

The swing fight

Roberto Soldić is a heavy favorite making his UFC debut having fought once since the middle of 2023, carrying a layoff and a new weight cut into a card at roughly 4,300 feet of altitude. Khaos Williams has never been knocked out and has five knockouts in seven UFC wins.

Williams at $7,000 has a 20th-percentile outcome of six points and an 80th-percentile outcome of ninety-two. That is the widest gap on the slate relative to price. If you are entering one lineup you have to pick a side rather than hedge, and the side with the unpriced uncertainty is the one whose opponent has not fought in two years.

What the model gets right and where it still misses

One check worth stating, because it is the only reason to trust any of the above. Tracked results from pro MMA DFS play show that roughly seventy percent of optimal lineups contain exactly one five-round fighter, about twenty-five percent contain neither, and about five percent contain both. Our simulation produces 71.8 percent, 28.2 percent, and zero.

The first two match. The third does not, and the reason is instructive: stacking both sides of a fight guarantees one loss, which only becomes optimal when so many favorites win that six winners cannot fit under the cap. Our model generates a six-winner optimal lineup 93.7 percent of the time, so it almost never produces the scenario that makes stacking correct. That is a known blind spot, it costs us about five percent of outcomes, and we are not going to paper over it.

We also do not have real ownership before lock. Nobody does. Every ownership figure here is modelled, labelled as modelled on our slate page, and tested against five different assumptions about how the field builds.

Duplication is the thing nobody prices

Our highest-scoring roster by raw simulation was heavily chalked — Smith, Wint, McGhee and the obvious value plays, with projected ownership summing to 214 percent where an average six-man roster lands near 129.

Then we asked how many other entries in a twenty-thousand-person field would build that exact lineup, and the answer was enough to cut its value roughly in half. Winning a tournament outright and splitting first place four ways are very different outcomes, and a model that only maximises score will walk you straight into the second one.

Adjusting for that reshuffled the board entirely and produced a lineup with ownership summing to 160 percent and an expected 0.6 duplicates instead of several.

The lineup

Jacobe Smith, Alexander Hernandez, Natalia Silva, Mick Parkin, Alden Coria, Khaos Williams. Exactly $50,000.

Silva is the five-round discount. Parkin and Coria are the two sides of the control-time trade — one banks position, the other cycles takedowns. Williams is the volatility. Hernandez is the mid-priced favorite nobody is talking about. Smith is simply the best projection on the card, and he is affordable because the cheap fighters on this slate can actually score rather than being fed to finishers.

Marcus McGhee is the notable omission, and he is omitted on purpose. He is a genuinely underpriced favorite who will be in roughly half the field. Those are the fighters you take in cash games and leave out of tournaments.
