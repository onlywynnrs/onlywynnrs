UFC 332 DFS Picks: Why the Five-Round Main Event Is the Cheapest Thing on the Board

DraftKings prices MMA fighters almost entirely off the moneyline. That is not a secret, it is how the format works, and it means every bout on a card prices out to the same pair total. On this card that number is $16,200, and thirteen of the fourteen bouts hit it exactly.

One does not. That is a real edge and we will get to it. But it is not the biggest thing on this slate.

Where the points actually come from

Before any of the analysis, a decomposition. We ran sixty thousand simulations and then broke each fighter's average score into its parts, because it is very easy to build a thesis on a scoring category that turns out to be worth four points.

Two things fall out of that table immediately.

The win and finish bonus is the biggest single category for almost everybody, which is why moneylines drive salary in the first place. And significant strikes are the only category where one fighter separates from the field by a wide margin.

Natalia Silva earns 37.6 points from striking alone. That is more than double what anyone else on this card produces, and it is the entire reason she is the most valuable piece on the slate.

The five-round discount

Silva and Wang Cong are the only two fighters here who get twenty-five minutes. They cost $8,700 and $7,500.

DraftKings cannot charge extra for championship rounds, because salary tracks the moneyline and the moneyline says nothing about scheduled length. So the two fighters with sixty-seven percent more scoring time than everyone else are priced as though they have the same fifteen minutes as a prelim fighter.

A significant strike is worth 0.4 points — it counts once as a strike and again as a significant strike. Silva lands about four and a half of them a minute. Ten extra minutes is therefore roughly eighteen points of pure striking, before control, before takedowns, before the bonus. She absorbs only 2.36 significant strikes a minute, defends sixty-six percent of what comes at her, and arrives on a fourteen-fight run with wins over three former champions.

Her likeliest outcome is a five-round decision. In this scoring system that is not a consolation prize, it is a top-five score. She appears in forty-two percent of our optimal lineups, clear of every expensive favorite on the card.

An earlier version of this article argued the opposite — that her ceiling sat below the mid-priced favorites and that Cong was the better side of the fight. That came out of a model that had significant strikes at 0.2 instead of 0.4 and no control-time term at all. Correcting the scoring engine moved her from the middle of the board to the top of it. The error was ours and it changed the conclusion.

The takedown ceiling, and why it matters less than it should

Here is a genuine structural quirk of this scoring system that almost nobody prices correctly.

A takedown is worth 5 points. Control time is worth 0.03 per second, which is 1.8 a minute. So one takedown equals 2.78 minutes of control. And you cannot land a second takedown until your opponent stands back up, which caps takedowns at one per round plus however many times they get up.

The consequence is counterintuitive: dominant wrestling is worse for fantasy than scrappy wrestling. A fighter who takes you down and holds you there banks control at 1.8 a minute. A fighter who takes you down, lets you up, and does it again banks 5 points every time. The crossover is not close — a one-takedown holder would need roughly 574 seconds of control per takedown to match a three-and-a-half-takedown cycler, and there are only 900 seconds in a fight.

So the best takedown scorers are not the best wrestlers. They are competent wrestlers facing someone who keeps standing up, which usually means a lighter weight class, where cardio allows repeated attempts and lower bodyweight makes holding position harder.

Alden Coria at $7,600 is that archetype here. Six takedowns across three UFC wins, a bantamweight who cycles position rather than smothering it, and a bout our model expects to go the full fifteen minutes. He produces 9.9 points of takedown scoring, second only to Jacobe Smith on the entire card, at a price near the bottom of the board.

Now the honest caveat, because the table above demands one. Control time itself is a small category — between two and seven points of mean score for everybody on this slate. The ceiling on takedowns only meaningfully binds for one fighter, Smith, and only in twelve percent of simulations. The mechanism is real, it belongs in the model, and it correctly identifies Coria. It is a tiebreaker, not a thesis, and anyone telling you control time is the hidden key to this slate is selling you something.

The bout that got repriced

Benardo Sopaj withdrew injured. Anthony Romero came in on three days' notice and the fight moved up to featherweight. Marcus McGhee was around -150 against Sopaj. He is roughly -500 against Romero.

This is the most reliable edge in MMA DFS: salaries are set early and do not move, so when an opponent changes and win probability jumps, the price is stale by definition. DraftKings partially adjusted, repricing the pair to $15,200 against $16,200 everywhere else, but a -500 favorite at $8,500 is still the cheapest shot at that tier by about $800.

The catch is that McGhee does not finish, and his fight is favored to go past two and a half rounds. In a format paying 90 for a first-round stoppage and 30 for a decision, that caps him. He also projects as the most-rostered fighter on the slate — roughly forty-seven percent ownership against thirty-three percent of optimal lineups.

He is underpriced and over-owned simultaneously. That makes him a cash-game play and a tournament fade, and those are different contests.

The swing fight

Roberto Soldić is a heavy favorite making his UFC debut having fought once since the middle of 2023, carrying a layoff and a new weight cut into a card at roughly 4,300 feet. Khaos Williams has never been knocked out and has five knockouts in seven UFC wins.

Williams at $7,000 has a 20th-percentile outcome of six points and an 80th-percentile outcome of ninety-two. That is the widest gap on the slate relative to price. Entering one lineup means picking a side rather than hedging, and the side with the unpriced uncertainty is the one whose opponent has not fought in two years.

Duplication is the thing nobody prices

Our highest-scoring roster by raw simulation was heavily chalked, with projected ownership summing to 214 percent where an average six-man roster lands near 129.

Then we asked how many entries in a twenty-thousand-person field would build that exact lineup. The answer was 4.7. Winning a tournament outright and splitting first place five ways are very different outcomes, and a model that only maximises score walks you into the second one.

Adjusting for duplication cut that roster's expected value by more than half and reshuffled the board. The build below carries ownership summing to 160 percent and an expected 0.6 duplicates.

What the model still gets wrong

One check, because it is the only reason to trust any of this. Tracked results from professional MMA DFS play show roughly seventy percent of optimal lineups contain exactly one five-round fighter, about twenty-five percent contain neither, and about five percent contain both. Our simulation produces 71.8 percent, 28.2 percent, and zero.

The first two match. The third does not. Stacking both sides of a fight guarantees one loss, which only becomes optimal when so many favorites win that six winners cannot fit under the cap. Our model finds a six-winner optimal lineup 93.7 percent of the time, so it almost never generates the scenario that makes stacking correct. That is a known blind spot worth about five percent of outcomes.

We also do not have real ownership before lock. Nobody does. Every ownership figure here is modelled, labelled as modelled on our slate page, and tested against five different assumptions about how the field builds.

Parkin is in because he is a live heavyweight underdog at $7,800 whose win-bonus equity is underpriced — heavyweight fights end, and an $7,800 fighter at +115 who wins is immediately in the optimal lineup. He also leads the cheap tier in control time, though as the table above shows, that is worth about four and a half points and is not the reason he is here.

Marcus McGhee is the deliberate omission. Underpriced, correct, and in roughly half the field.

One scheduling note that matters more than it sounds: fighters lock at the start time of the first fight on the card, not at their own fight. Everything locks together when the early prelims begin. Late news after that point cannot be pivoted off.
