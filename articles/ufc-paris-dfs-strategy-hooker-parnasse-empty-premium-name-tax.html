UFC Paris DFS: Reading a Card Where Half the Numbers Lie

Two corrections before the analysis, because both change the conclusions.

First, last week we identified Song Yadong as the leverage play against Umar Nurmagomedov in Shanghai and then recommended a lineup with Nurmagomedov in it. Song knocked him out. That is in the tracker as a loss.

Second, the original version of this article leaned almost entirely on fantasy points per fight, and that was a mistake serious enough to warrant rewriting the whole thing rather than patching it.

The denominator problem

Fantasy points per fight is an average. Averages need a sample, and on this card most of them do not have one.

Kurtis Campbell is priced at $9,300 with a 20.3 average. He has fought in the UFC exactly once: a main card assignment in London earlier this year where he faltered. That 20.3 is one bad night, divided by one.

Losene Keita is $9,200 with a 20.8 average. He has also fought once, a competitive decision loss to Nathaniel Wood in March. One night, divided by one.

Now the same test in the other direction. Luis Felipe Dias carries a 108.5 average, the highest per-dollar figure on the board. He has also fought once, a first-round stoppage of YiSak Lee at the end of May. A quick finish generates a big number and dividing it by one keeps it big.

Three fighters, three single-fight samples, and they sit at the two extremes of the slate. Reading the low ones as proof of weakness and the high one as proof of value is not analysis. It is the same noise, sorted by sign.

The fighters on this card who actually have samples worth reading are the ones nobody is excited about. Muhammad Naimov has gone 5-2 through seven UFC appearances and is 2-2 in his last four. Dan Hooker is a veteran of 24 UFC fights. Nora Cornolle split her first six. Those records tell you something. A number derived from one fight tells you what happened that night.

Debutants are not blank slates

The other error was calling seven fighters unknowable because DraftKings shows a dash. They have careers. They just happened elsewhere.

Salahdine Parnasse is 23-2 with 15 finishes, a two-division KSW champion with seven successful title defenses between featherweight and lightweight, and his only setback since 2023 came trying for a belt in a third weight class. He is a 28-year-old boxer by base with a 73-inch reach, and he knocked out Kenneth Cross in the first round in California in May. That is a deeper competitive record than several fighters on this card who do have UFC averages.

Pavel Andrusca is 8-0 with seven finishes, five of them in the first round. He is also a short-notice replacement for Mairon Santos, who withdrew ill, which cuts against him in a way his record does not show.

Michael Aljarouj brings a 13-3 mark and a seven-fight unbeaten run into a debut at home. Fabia Sintes has won seven straight and defended a WOW flyweight title twice. Matthieu Duclos lost on the Contender Series in Season 7 and has since won four in a row to earn the call in his home country.

None of that is a blank line. Some of it is more informative than a one-fight UFC average.

Nine French fighters and a building that has history

Parnasse, Ziam, Sola, Charriere, Cornolle, Sy, Aljarouj, Duclos, and Benouaich all fight at home on Saturday. That is nine of twenty-eight, and Accor Arena is not a neutral room.

The home effect is usually hand-waved. Here it is measurable for specific fighters. Morgan Charriere has fought twice at Accor Arena and stopped his opponent both times, the best results of his UFC career. Fares Ziam is 2-0 in Paris, including a knockout of Matt Frevola, and returns home for the first time in three years. Nora Cornolle has fought there twice as well, though she arrives on a two-fight skid after a second-round stoppage loss to Joselyne Edwards.

For fantasy purposes, what matters is that finishes score and this building has produced finishes from these particular fighters. That is a narrower and more defensible claim than "the crowd helps."

The matchups that actually decide scoring

Michael Page versus Nursulton Ruziboev is the clearest read on the card, and it is a style call rather than a sample call. Page is 39, elusive, and built to avoid exchanges. His most recent outing was a low-output decision over Sam Patterson. His fantasy average is modest not because of small samples but because his entire method is to not be hit and to not over-commit, which produces exactly the kind of fight that scores poorly. Ruziboev started his UFC run with stoppages of Brunno Ferreira and Sedriques Dumas, has three straight wins since returning to middleweight, and finished Andrey Pulyaev by technical submission in June. A finisher priced below an avoider is a fantasy edge regardless of who the market favors.

Daniil Donchenko versus Punahele Soriano is the most interesting fight for tournament purposes. Donchenko is a 25-year-old TUF winner who leads with elbows and does damage, but the UFC's own preview notes his willingness to stand in the pocket puts him in harm's way. Soriano has won four straight at welterweight, has a good chin, real power, and grappling to fall back on. A fighter who takes risks in the pocket against a durable puncher is a coin flip that the price does not treat like one.

Kurtis Campbell versus Trevor Peek should not be read off the averages at all. Peek has been out nearly two years with injuries, is on a two-fight skid, and fights all gas and no brakes. Two years of inactivity is a real fantasy risk that no per-fight number captures. Campbell's single bad night is not proof he is bad, and Peek's aggression is not proof he is live. This is the fight on the card with the least reliable information on either side, which is a reason to avoid it rather than a reason to be clever in it.

Losene Keita versus Muhammad Naimov carries a genuine cross-reference. Keita's only UFC appearance was a loss to Nathaniel Wood, who fights on this same card. Naimov was submitted by Felipe Lima, who also fights on this card. Two fighters, two shared opponents in the building, and Naimov has six more Octagon appearances of evidence. Worth noting Keita missed weight by four pounds in Paris last year and had his debut cancelled outright; he made 146 cleanly this time, which is a small positive rather than a lingering red flag.

Mario Pinto versus Ryan Spann deserves a caveat. Pinto is unbeaten with three wins since Contender Series Season 8, but his most recent outing against Felipe Franco was underwhelming given Franco is a natural light heavyweight who took the fight short notice. Spann lost his heavyweight debut and has since stopped Lukasz Brzeski and Marcus Buchecha. Heavyweight is the division where a live underdog's path is shortest.

The main event

Hooker is 24 UFC fights deep, on a two-fight skid after losses to Arman Tsarukyan and Benoit Saint Denis, and enters his fourth UFC main event. Parnasse is a debutant handed five rounds, which almost never happens.

The UFC's own preview names the dynamic honestly: people underrate the battle-tested veteran and inflate expectations for the hyped newcomer. That is a statement about ownership as much as about the fight.

What it means for a lineup is narrower than it looks. Five rounds is two extra rounds of scoring opportunity for whoever is winning, and both men finish fights. Hooker is cheap and experienced against elite competition. Parnasse is expensive, has finished 15 of 23, and is fighting in front of a crowd that came to see him. Both are defensible. Neither is a value play in the sense of a number being wrong.

What we are doing with this

We are rebuilding our projections to weight by sample size rather than treating every average as equal, because our own optimizer produced a lineup off unweighted numbers and that lineup was not built on what we actually believe.

The reads we are confident in are the style ones. Ruziboev over Page on output. Soriano live against a fighter who takes damage to deal it. Avoid the Campbell and Peek fight entirely. Respect Naimov's sample over Keita's single data point. And treat Accor Arena as a real variable for Charriere and Ziam specifically, not as a vague home-field gesture.
