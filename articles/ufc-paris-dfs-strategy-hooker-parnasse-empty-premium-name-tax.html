UFC Paris DFS: Nine Fighters at Home and the Matchups That Decide the Slate

Fourteen fights at Accor Arena, prelims at noon ET, main card at three. Everyone made weight. Nine of the twenty-eight fighters are French and fighting at home, which is the single largest structural feature of this card and the one most likely to be underweighted.

Here is how the slate breaks down.

The main event is a stylistic mismatch in both directions

Salahdine Parnasse is 23-2 with 15 finishes. He held KSW gold at both featherweight and lightweight and defended those belts seven times combined. His only recent loss came chasing a third title in a third weight class. He is 28, boxing-based, carries a 73-inch reach, and knocked out Kenneth Cross in the first round in California in May. The UFC gave him a five-round main event on debut, which is close to unheard of.

Dan Hooker is 24 UFC fights deep, entering his fourth UFC main event, and on a two-fight skid after losses to Arman Tsarukyan and Benoit Saint Denis. His striking is still excellent and his list of opponents is a murderer's row.

The UFC's own preview names the dynamic bluntly: crowds underrate the battle-tested veteran and inflate the debutant. That is a statement about ownership before it is a statement about the fight.

For lineup purposes, five rounds is the fact that matters. Two extra rounds is two extra rounds of strikes, takedown attempts, and control for whoever is ahead. Both men finish. Hooker at $6,700 is the cheapest path on the card to a five-round ceiling, and Parnasse at $9,500 is the most expensive fighter on the board being priced on hype that may well be justified. Neither is a mispricing. The difference is that one costs $2,800 less.

Michael Page versus Nursulton Ruziboev is the clearest read on the card

Page is 39 and returns to middleweight on a three-fight winning streak that includes wins over Shara Magomedov and Jared Cannonier. He is still slick. He is also, by design, an avoider — his last outing was a low-output decision over Sam Patterson in London, and low output is what his style produces by intent, not by accident. He wins by not being hit and not over-committing.

Ruziboev opened his UFC run with stoppages of Brunno Ferreira and Sedriques Dumas, went to welterweight and lost a decision to Joaquin Buckley, then returned to middleweight and won three straight, most recently a technical submission of Andrey Pulyaev in June.

A finisher priced $1,200 below an avoider is the kind of gap that does not need a projection model to see. Page can absolutely win this fight. He is unlikely to win it in a way that scores.

Donchenko versus Soriano is the best fight nobody is talking about

Daniil Donchenko is 25, won TUF Season 33, and has three UFC wins — Rodrigo Sezinando at Noche UFC, then Alex Moreno and Theodor Berggren. He leads with elbows and does real damage. The UFC's preview also notes that his willingness to stand in the pocket puts him in harm's way, which is a polite way of saying he trades.

Punahele Soriano has won four straight since moving to welterweight, most recently a hard fight with Ramiz Brahimaj in February. He is an Xtreme Couture product with a good chin, genuine power, and grappling to fall back on if the striking goes badly.

A fighter who takes damage to deal it, against a durable puncher on a four-fight run, priced at minus 240 and plus 182. That gap is wider than the fight is. Soriano is the live underdog of the slate.

Two fights to leave alone

Kurtis Campbell versus Trevor Peek. Campbell is a Contender Series graduate from Liverpool who got a main card slot in London earlier this year and faltered. Peek has been out nearly two years with injuries, is on a two-fight skid, and fights all gas and no brakes. Neither man has given you reliable recent information. Two years of inactivity is a real risk and one bad night is not a verdict. There is no read here, only a guess dressed as one.

Aljarouj versus Sintes. Michael Aljarouj is 13-3 with a no-contest and a seven-fight unbeaten run, debuting at home. Fabia Sintes has won seven straight and twice defended a WOW flyweight title. Both are legitimate. Both are debuting. The line is a pick'em because the market cannot separate them either, and neither can we.

Keita versus Naimov has the best cross-reference on the card

Losene Keita was a double champion in OKTAGON and was supposed to debut in Paris last year, but missed weight by four pounds and the Patricio Pitbull fight was scrapped. He debuted in March instead and lost a competitive decision to Nathaniel Wood — who fights on this same card, on this same night. He made 146 cleanly this week, which resolves the weight question rather than leaving it hanging.

Muhammad Naimov is 5-2 across seven UFC appearances and 2-2 in his last four. He was submitted by Felipe Lima, who is also on this card.

That is two shared opponents in the same building, which is about as much real signal as a mid-card fight ever gives you. Naimov has six more Octagon fights of evidence than Keita does, and the market has him at plus 284. That price is paying for Keita's reputation from Europe, not for what either man has shown in the UFC.

Home cage, specifically

The home-crowd argument is usually hand-waved. On this card it is concrete for two fighters.

Morgan Charriere has fought twice at Accor Arena and stopped his opponent both times — the best results of his UFC career. He faces Felipe Lima, who submitted Naimov on short notice, beat Miles Johns, and lost a competitive decision to Payton Talbott before returning to featherweight. Charriere at plus 160 in the building where he has produced two finishes is a defensible tournament play.

Fares Ziam is 2-0 in Paris with a knockout of Matt Frevola, fighting at home for the first time in three years. He carried a six-fight winning streak into 2026 before Tom Nolan halted it in June. Across from him is Axel Sola, also French, three fights into his UFC run, who brawled with Mason Jones in March and submitted Ismael Bonfim in the first round in July. Two home fighters in the co-main means the crowd factor cancels; this one comes down to whether Ziam can slow it down or Sola can keep it a striking match.

The rest of the board, briefly

Mario Pinto versus Ryan Spann. Pinto is unbeaten with three wins since Contender Series Season 8, but his last outing against Felipe Franco was underwhelming given Franco is a natural light heavyweight who took it on short notice. Spann lost his heavyweight debut to Waldo Cortes Acosta, then stopped Lukasz Brzeski and Marcus Buchecha. Heavyweight is where an underdog's path is shortest, and Spann at plus 248 has stopped his last two.

Nathaniel Wood versus Pavel Andrusca. Wood is on a four-fight streak and has won seven of eight at featherweight. Andrusca is 8-0 with seven finishes, five in the first round — but he is a short-notice replacement for Mairon Santos, who pulled out ill. A finisher's record is worth respecting; a full camp is worth more.

Oumar Sy versus Modestas Bukauskas. Sy won three of his first four before getting caught in a submission last time out. Bukauskas lost to Nikita Krylov in January and took a split decision from Christian Edwards. Both are coming off results that raise questions.

Cornolle versus Sygula. Cornolle split her first six UFC fights, has fought twice at Accor, and arrives off a second-round stoppage loss to Joselyne Edwards. Sygula has won two straight including a win over Priscila Cachoeira and is priced at even money. Momentum favors the Pole.

Duclos versus Dias. Matthieu Duclos lost on Contender Series Season 7 and has won four straight to earn this call at home. Luis Felipe Dias earned his contract last season and stopped YiSak Lee in the first round in May. A near pick'em between a home debutant on a four-fight run and a recent finisher. Genuinely close.

Game theory

The contest takes 14,705 entries and six fighters, and it locks at noon ET.

Ownership will concentrate in predictable places: Parnasse because he is the story of the night and the highest salary; the Contender Series and TUF names with gaudy single-fight numbers attached; and whichever heavy favorite the projection sites lead with. Recognizable names in a home-country main event get owned more than their price implies, not less.

The differentiation on this card is not in going contrarian for its own sake. It is in the fights where a style read beats a reputation — Ruziboev over Page on output, Soriano's chin against Donchenko's willingness to trade, Naimov's seven fights of evidence against Keita's one, Charriere in the building where he has finished twice.

And it is in declining to play the two fights where nobody has real information. Skipping a bad fight is a decision, and on a fourteen-fight slate you can afford to make it twice.
