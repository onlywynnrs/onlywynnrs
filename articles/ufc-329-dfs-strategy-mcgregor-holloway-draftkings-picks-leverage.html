UFC 329 DFS Strategy: McGregor vs Holloway DraftKings Picks & Leverage

UFC 329, Final Look: Weigh-Ins Clean, Lines Moving

Weigh-ins are done, all 28 fighters made weight, and the board has moved hard since fight week opened. This is the updated read for Saturday night at T-Mobile Arena — what changed, what it means for DraftKings lineups, and where the leverage now sits. The scoring math is unchanged: first-round finish +90, decision +30. Roster finishers.

We don't sell certainty — anyone claiming to know how a McGregor comeback goes is guessing with confidence. Here's the final map.

The Main Event: The Trap Got Worse

When we first wrote this card up, Holloway was -225 and McGregor +185, with the line having already moved from a -550 open. It kept going: McGregor is now around +138, an implied 42%. That's five years of pent-up name money compressing the price all week.

The weigh-in note that matters: Holloway hit 170 on the nose for his first-ever walk at welterweight and looked comfortable doing it — no brutal cut, which was the one physical question about the move up. McGregor came in at 170.5, on point for his first fight in five years.

The DFS translation hasn't changed — it's intensified. McGregor's ownership will now be even further above his real win probability, because the shrinking line reads like confirmation to casual entrants. He is the single most negative-leverage play on the slate. Holloway at roughly 68%, with the highest-volume output of his era across a possible 25 minutes, is the quietly correct side — and the more the line tightens, the more correct being on Max at low relative ownership becomes.

The Co-Main Flipped

The biggest market story on the card: Paddy Pimblett opened this fight a +114 underdog. As of Friday, some books have him favored, with the pair sitting in a genuine pick'em band. That's a full flip on Benoit Saint Denis inside fight week.

The violence read is unchanged — the under is still heavily juiced, and the market expects a finish either way. But the leverage side switched. Ownership tends to lag line movement: much of the field will still see BSD as "the favorite on a four-finish streak" while the market no longer prices him that way. If the flip is right, BSD at fading ownership is now the sharp side of the same violence — the exact opposite of our early-week lean. That's not us hedging; that's what the line did.

Dogs Closing Everywhere

The other theme of fight week: the underdogs got bet, across the card.

McKinney vs. Green is now dead even — pick'em at most shops after opening McKinney -148. The market is saying this coin-flip of violence is a true coin-flip, and Green's side still carries less ownership.

Whittaker vs. Krylov tightened to roughly -113/+113. Krylov — whose fights essentially never see a scorecard — is now a near-coinflip priced like a dog in DFS salary terms. He remains the cleanest live-dog leverage on the board.

Riley vs. Kamaka moved from -290 to roughly -200. Costa vs. Durden did the same. Those are the two spots our sharp-money model flagged as dog steam earlier this week, and the closing numbers confirm it: real money came in on Kamaka and Durden. Riley (13-0) and short-notice Costa are still favored, but the market's confidence in both softened noticeably.

Steveson vs. Ellison is the last mover: the Olympic champion's number came down from the -2800 neighborhood to nearer -1100. Still overwhelming — roughly 92% — but the market walked back some of the debut euphoria. He weighed in at a fit 241 to Ellison's 236. The GPP logic holds: everyone plays him, so the deliberate fade remains the slate's biggest leverage lever, now with slightly better justification.

What Didn't Move

Yanez vs. Garbrandt (~-350) remains the cleanest finish-hunting chalk: a sharp boxer against a chin that's been cracked repeatedly. Pinas vs. Almeida actually moved toward Pinas — the one favorite the market strengthened on. Sandhagen vs. Bautista stayed in the -130 range and stays a decision-risk trap for DFS: elite fight, mediocre scoring profile. Cortez vs. Wang Cong remains a true pick'em, an ownership-splitter where either side is fine.

The Final Build Logic

Anchor with finishers whose lines held or strengthened (Yanez, Pinas, Steveson if you'll eat the chalk). Treat the flipped co-main honestly: BSD is now the leverage side of a guaranteed-violence fight. Respect what the market said all week about the dogs — Krylov, Green, Kamaka, and Durden all closed stronger than they opened, and all carry finish equity at low ownership. And in tournaments, remember the two crowd magnets: McGregor and Steveson will soak up the field's exposure. Being deliberately different in exactly those two places, and chalk everywhere else, is the classic winning shape for a card like this.

Every roster spot needs a realistic path to a finish. That's the whole game.
