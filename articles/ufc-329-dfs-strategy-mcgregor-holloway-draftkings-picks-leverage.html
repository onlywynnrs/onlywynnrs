UFC 329 DFS Strategy: McGregor vs Holloway DraftKings Picks & Leverage

UFC 329 Is a Chalk-and-Chaos Slate

International Fight Week gives us the biggest DFS slate of the summer: McGregor vs. Holloway 2 headlining a 14-fight card at T-Mobile Arena. For DraftKings MMA purposes, this card splits cleanly into two groups — a handful of massive favorites the whole field will roster, and a cluster of genuine coin-flips where tournaments will be won. The scoring math never changes: a first-round finish is worth +90, a decision just +30. Your job is to find the six fighters most likely to end fights, and to be different from the field where being different is actually justified.

We don't sell certainty — anyone claiming to know how a McGregor comeback goes is guessing with confidence. Here's how we read the board.

The Main Event: A Genuine DFS Puzzle

Holloway is around -225 (a 69% implied favorite) with McGregor at +185 — and here's the market note that matters: the line opened as high as -550 and has moved hard toward McGregor, driven by heavy public money on the name. Roughly 68% of the handle is on Holloway, but this fight is drawing 3.5x more money than anything else on the card, and name-brand money distorts lines.

The DFS translation: McGregor will be massively owned relative to his real win probability, because casual entrants roster the star. That makes him a negative leverage play — if you want the upset, you'll share it with a huge chunk of the field. Holloway at 69% to win, fresh off knockout form and far more active, is the quietly correct side. The honest caveat: Holloway is moving up to 170 for the first time, and five-round main events reward the higher-volume fighter — which has been Holloway's entire career identity. Volume plus finish threat in a five-rounder is exactly the profile that posts slate-winning scores.

The Mega-Chalk Question: Gable Steveson

The Olympic gold medalist debuts at a staggering -2800 against Elisha Ellison, with the under 1.5 rounds juiced to -600 — the market expects a fast, dominant finish. Everyone will play him. The game-theory question isn't whether he wins; it's whether a debut heavyweight wrestler finishes fast enough to beat the slate's other scores. Our honest read: you probably can't fade him in cash, but in large-field tournaments, a deliberate pivot off the single highest-owned fighter on the card is one of the few legitimate leverage sources available — you just need the fighters you pivot to to finish.

Where the Finishes Live

Saint Denis vs. Pimblett is the co-main and the card's clearest violence signal: the under 2.5 rounds is heavily juiced, meaning the market expects a finish either way. Saint Denis (-135) is on a four-fight streak with finishes of Dan Hooker and Beneil Dariush; Pimblett (+114) is coming off his first UFC loss. Both directions carry ceiling here — this is a fight to have exposure to on both sides across tournament entries, weighted toward BSD.

McKinney vs. Green is built for chaos. Terrance McKinney (-148) is one of the fastest starters in the sport — his fights tend to end in minutes, one way or the other. That's the boom-or-bust profile that wins GPPs and zeroes cash lineups. King Green (+124) is a live dog with real power. Either fighter can post a +25 quick-win bonus; neither is safe.

Yanez vs. Garbrandt: Adrian Yanez (-410) against a former champion whose recent knockout losses have pushed him to +320. Yanez is a boxer with finishing instincts against a chin that's been cracked repeatedly — a clean finish-hunting spot at a premium price. Garbrandt's counter-power makes him a thin dart, nothing more.

Riley vs. Kamaka: Luke Riley (-290) brings a 13-0 record and heavy hype. Undefeated prospects in showcase spots are exactly what this scoring format rewards — until they aren't. He's a strong ceiling play; just remember the field will see the same thing.

The Technicians — Handle With Care

Sandhagen vs. Bautista (-148/+124) is the best pure fight on the card and a DFS trap. Both are elite, both are durable, and technical bantamweight chess frequently goes fifteen minutes. A Sandhagen decision is a fine real-world result and a mediocre fantasy score. Bautista's wrestling-pace style can rack up takedown points, which gives him a sneaky decision-based floor at underdog ownership — a legitimately contrarian angle.

Whittaker vs. Krylov (-142/+120): Whittaker at light heavyweight against Krylov, whose fights almost never see the scorecards. Krylov's all-finish profile makes him live-dog leverage; Whittaker's class makes him the safer side. Another both-sides tournament fight.

Royval vs. Kavanagh (-218 Kavanagh): flyweights bring pace and scrambles. Royval (+180) has never been in a boring fight, and his chaos style generates points even in losses — but losses still cap you. Kavanagh is the rising favorite fresh off the hype train.

Cortez vs. Wang Cong is a true pick'em (-110/-110 range) between two grapplers. Coin-flip fights are ownership-splitters: whoever you pick, roughly half the field disagrees with you, which makes either side fine leverage. Lean toward whichever style you trust to score — control time and takedowns add up.

The Short-Notice Spots

This card lost two fighters to injury late, and both replacements matter for DFS.

Farid Basharat (-600) now faces late-replacement John Garza after Ethyn Ewing withdrew. Short-notice opponents at long odds are finish-rate accelerators — the undefeated Basharat against a fill-in is quiet chalk that the casual field, distracted by the main event, may underweight. These unglamorous prelim hammers are often the highest floor-per-dollar spots on a pay-per-view slate.

Costa vs. Durden is the stranger version of the same story. Alessandro Costa stepped in on roughly two weeks' notice for the injured Ode Osbourne — and the market still made him a -250 favorite over Cody Durden. That almost never happens with replacements, and it reflects Costa's form: two TKO finishes already in 2026, the latest ending Matt Schnell in under three minutes. Durden, a durable veteran who never turns down a fight, just snapped a four-fight skid in April. The honest DFS read: Costa's finishing form makes him a legitimate mid-card anchor, but a short camp cuts both ways — if Durden drags him into deep water, the abbreviated preparation is the variable nobody can price. Costa early, Durden late is the shape of this one.

The Build

The framework for a card like this: pick your lane on the main event knowing McGregor will be overowned relative to his chances; decide whether you're eating the Steveson chalk or making the leverage pivot; get exposure to the guaranteed-violence fights (BSD/Pimblett, McKinney/Green); and fill with finishers, not decision-grinders. In cash games, lean on Holloway, Steveson, Basharat, and Yanez. In tournaments, mix in the live dogs with real finishing equity — Krylov, Green, Bautista's grinding floor — and let one contrarian call separate you from the field.

Every roster spot needs a realistic path to a finish. That's the whole game.
