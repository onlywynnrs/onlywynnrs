UFC Abu Dhabi DFS Game Theory: Ankalaev vs Guskov $300K Throwdown Guide

UFC Abu Dhabi Game Theory: A Chalk-Saturated Card and the $50K Squeeze

The UFC's return to Etihad Arena this Saturday (early card — main card starts noon ET) is a 13-fight slate with a structural quirk that defines the whole tournament: seven favorites are priced -310 or heavier. That sounds like a safe card. For DFS, it's the opposite of simple — because your $50,000 cap physically cannot hold all the safety. The real game this week isn't picking winners; it's predicting which chalk the field crowds into, and pairing the chalk you keep with underdogs who actually score. Here's the full game-theory read for the $300K Throwdown (~19,600 entries, $100K to first).

The Structural Problem: Too Much Chalk for One Cap

Dulatov ($9,800, -900), Tuchalov ($9,700, -1099), Ankalaev ($9,500, -550), Patterson ($9,400), Hussein ($9,300), Zaynukov ($9,200), Kuniev ($9,000, -350). Seven heavy favorites, all priced $9,000+. Fit three of them and you've spent $28,000+ of your cap with three spots left. That math forces every entrant to choose their chalk — which means ownership fragments across the top of the board rather than concentrating on one Steveson-style lock. When chalk ownership fragments, the leverage math changes: you gain less by fading any single favorite, and you gain more by nailing the underdogs who fill the other three spots. This is a "win the bottom half of your lineup" card.

The Main Event: Ankalaev's Redemption vs. the 18-Finish Replacement

Magomed Ankalaev (-550) returns from the worst moment of his career — an 80-second knockout against Alex Pereira in October, the first stoppage loss he'd ever taken. Bogdan Guskov took this fight on two weeks' notice when Khalil Rountree withdrew, and here's the stat that matters for this format: all 18 of Guskov's career wins are finishes. He does not win decisions. At $6,700 near the bottom of the salary sheet, with a 78.3 FPPF that's actually higher than Ankalaev's 77.3, Guskov is the classic min-priced dog whose only winning path is the one DraftKings pays most.

The honest read: this is a substantial step up for Guskov, on a short camp, against a wrestler whose control game is built to neutralize exactly his forward-pressure style. Ankalaev's DFS problem is different — he's historically not a high-volume finisher, and a five-round control win scores fine, not huge. The analysts see this as Ankalaev's spot to finally get a stoppage against a hittable opponent stepping up in class. Both reads can be true: Ankalaev is safe chalk with a better-than-usual finish shot; Guskov at 20% is a pure ceiling dart at near-zero relative cost, and his all-finish profile makes him one of the few sub-$7K fighters with a genuine slate-winning outcome.

The Best Fight for DFS: Erceg vs. Temirov

A true pick'em (-112/-113) — and the salary sheet leans the wrong way. Steve Erceg costs $8,200 with a 64.5 FPPF; Ramazan Temirov costs $8,000 with a 93.9 FPPF — nearly 30 points more output for $200 less, in a dead-even fight. Temirov is undersized with a reach disadvantage and compensates with exactly what this scoring format rewards: explosive speed and relentless aggression. In a coinflip, take the volume side at the discount. Temirov is the best pure value on the card, and because the field's attention lives on the heavy favorites, he'll be under-owned for a 50/50 fighter with elite output.

The Verified-Output Dogs: Where the Bottom Half Gets Won

Remember the structure: everyone's forced to roster underdogs. The edge is picking dogs with real scoring history instead of salary-relief bodies.

Mike Davis ($7,700, +176, 85.2 FPPF) is the headliner of this group. Aliev is an 11-0 wrestler with the crowd behind him, but Davis is the better striker and athlete, and multiple analysts are openly backing the upset. An 85-point average at underdog ownership in a genuinely winnable fight is the leverage profile that separates lineups.

Tyrell Fortune ($7,200, +255, 70.0 FPPF) — heavyweight power dog with a real average. Big men at plus-money end fights with one punch.

Thomas Petersen ($7,400, +159) against Valter Walker is the contrarian side of a heavyweight fight that ends early either way — though Walker ($8,800, 97.3 FPPF, -208) is himself one of the best value favorites on the board and our preferred side.

The trap in this tier: Santiago Ponzinibbio ($6,800, +360). A 31-9 veteran name the casual field recognizes, now 3-7 in his last ten and facing a rising finisher in Patterson. Name-brand dog ownership with fading physical tools — let the field carry it.

The Debutant Problem, Squared

Six fighters have no UFC scoring history — and one of them, Magomed Tuchalov, is priced at $9,700 as a -1099 favorite. Paying second-highest salary on the slate for a fighter with zero UFC data is the Steveson dilemma with less evidence: the market loves him, the sample is empty. Our standing doctrine caps unknowns at one per lineup, and prefers the one whose line moves toward him late. Watch the live board: Zaynukov (-300, 8-0) and Vagaev (-244) are the mid-priced debutant favorites where a late steam move would be a genuine signal. Rzepecki vs. Zaynukov is a double-debut fight — pure fog, and in a 19K field, fog you skip is variance your opponents eat.

The Build Doctrine

Pick two premium chalk pieces, not three — Dulatov's 108.9 FPPF backs his price better than Tuchalov's empty sample backs his. Anchor the mid-tier with the card's two best value plays (Temirov, Walker). Fill the bottom with output dogs (Davis first, Fortune or Guskov as the ceiling dart) instead of recognizable names. And remember what the fragmented-chalk math means: in this field, the winning lineup probably holds the same favorites as thousands of others — it wins on the two or three underdog spots. That's where your attention belongs this week.
