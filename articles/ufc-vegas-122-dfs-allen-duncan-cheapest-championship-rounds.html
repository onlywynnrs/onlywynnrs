UFC Vegas 122 DFS: Three Weight Misses, One Flipped Line, and a Changed Lineup

Updated Saturday, after weigh-ins. Three fighters missed weight, one line flipped entirely, and the weigh-in sheet corrected a weight class we had wrong. Our lineup changed. The original version of this piece and what moved are both below.

What the weigh-ins changed

Ismael Bonfim came in at 163.5 for a 155-pound bout — eight and a half pounds over, and the third weight miss of his UFC career. The bout proceeds with a purse forfeiture rather than as a catchweight.

The market reacted immediately. Bonfim had been a small favourite at around -110. He is now +100 and Francisco Prado is -120, which is a full flip. The fight total sits at over/under 1.5 rounds, meaning the market expects this to end fast, and Bonfim has been stopped in three straight fights with all seven of his career losses coming by knockout or submission.

Prado at $8,000 is now the clearest value on the card. He is a live favourite against an opponent who is depleted, repeatedly finishable, and expected to be finished early — and a first-round stoppage pays 90 points with another 25 if it comes inside sixty seconds. His projection moved up and he now shows the best leverage in our model at 1.21x, appearing in thirty-four percent of optimal lineups against twenty-eight percent projected ownership.

Ketlen Souza missed by two pounds and Brendson Ribeiro by three and a half. Both are minor, both carry small cardio penalties in our model, and neither changes a conclusion.

A correction on our side. The weigh-in sheet confirmed Harris vs Frye Jr. is a heavyweight bout — 261 pounds against 246. Our original model treated it as a lighter division, which understated the finish rate and knockdown rate for both men. Heavyweight fights end early and heavyweights hold position rather than cycling takedowns, so we rebuilt both fighters' profiles. Harris keeps roughly the same total projection but the shape of it changed substantially, with more coming from finishes and less from takedown volume.

The main event got tighter. Christian Leroy Duncan opened at +125 and has been bet all the way to -102. Brendan Allen moved from -140 to -120. Stripped of vig that is now 52/48, and Duncan is the second-best points-per-dollar play on the board behind only Allen.

We tested the stack and rejected it

With both main-event fighters ranking top-two in points per dollar, stacking them is the obvious thought. Twenty-five minutes from both sides of one fight, no favourite tax on either.

We ran it. A main-event stack grades at 0.262 percent to post a winning score, against 0.888 percent for the build below. It is not close.

The reason is that stacking guarantees one loss, and in our simulations ninety-seven percent of optimal lineups contain six winners. Spending $16,200 of a $50,000 cap to buy one guaranteed zero is a worse trade than the extra ten minutes is worth, and both fighters are heavily owned on top of that — a stacked pair pushes the roster's total projected ownership to 238 percent.

This is the scenario where stacking is supposed to make sense, and the numbers still say no. Worth knowing that the rule of thumb has a limit.

The original analysis

Every one of the twelve bouts on this card prices to exactly $16,200 per pair. No stale line this week, so the value has to be structural — and it is sitting in the main event, where the only two fighters scheduled for twenty-five minutes cost $8,500 and $7,700 in a near pick'em.

DraftKings cannot charge extra for championship rounds, because salary tracks the moneyline and the moneyline says nothing about scheduled length. What makes this week unusual is that neither main-event fighter carries a favourite tax on top of that.

Allen remains the best single piece on the slate: the highest ceiling at 133.8 and the best points per dollar on the board. Fourteen of his twenty-seven wins are submissions and he averages roughly two and a half takedown attempts per fifteen minutes, which is close to ideal here — takedowns pay 5 each, control pays 1.8 a minute, and a submission pays the full round bonus on top. He earns meaningfully in four scoring categories at once, and the clock is why.

Felipe Franco finished all ten of his pre-UFC fights inside the opening five minutes. At $9,300 he averages 58 points of bonus alone, more than some fighters on this card score in total, and his opponent now has the lowest floor on the slate at 3.3 points after missing weight.

Loopy Godinez is the expensive trap. At $9,000 and -244 she is the fourth-priciest fighter here, and eleven of her fourteen wins came by decision. Her floor is genuinely good, which makes her a reasonable cash piece, but you are paying near the top of the board for a fighter whose likeliest outcome is a 30-point bonus instead of a 90-point one. She is a hold in a double-up and a fade in a tournament.

Niko Price is fighting for the last time, at $7,500 with a 98-point ceiling. Retirement fights draw entries, and his projected ownership and optimal-lineup share are nearly identical, so there is no edge either way — just a good cheap ceiling that will not differentiate you.

Exactly $50,000.

Two changes from the version we posted yesterday: Matheus Camilo out for RJ Harris, and Melissa Gatto out for Francisco Prado. Both swaps are downstream of the weigh-ins — Harris because the heavyweight correction raised his finishing profile, Prado because his opponent came in eight and a half pounds heavy and the market flipped the fight. The new build grades at 0.888 percent to post a winning score against 0.728 percent for yesterday's, a twenty-two percent relative improvement.

Allen is the engine. Franco is the first-round bonus. Harris is a heavyweight favourite whose fight is likely to end. Prado is the weight-miss edge. Kamaka and Price are the cheap ceilings.

This is not a contrarian build and we will not dress it up as one — projected ownership across the six sums to about 215 percent, where a random six-man roster on a twenty-four-fighter slate lands near 150. We looked at lower-owned alternatives and they were worse on the only number that matters, which is the chance of posting a winning score. Taking a large-field tournament down with one lineup is a long shot by construction. The job is to maximise that chance given what the slate offers, not to minimise the odds of sharing the prize.

One scheduling note: fighters lock at the start time of the first fight on the card, not at their own fight. Everything locks together when the early prelims begin, so late news after that cannot be pivoted off.
