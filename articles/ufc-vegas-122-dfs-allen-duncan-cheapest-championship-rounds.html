UFC Vegas 122 DFS: The Cheapest Championship Rounds of the Year

Every one of the twelve bouts on this card prices to exactly $16,200 per pair. No exceptions, no late replacement, no stale line. Last week there was one repriced bout and it was the easiest edge on the board. This week that edge does not exist, so the value has to come from somewhere structural instead.

It does, and it is sitting in the main event.

A pick-em main event is the best thing that can happen to a DFS slate

Brendan Allen is -140. Christian Leroy Duncan is +112. They are the only two fighters on this card scheduled for twenty-five minutes, and they cost $8,500 and $7,700.

DraftKings cannot charge extra for championship rounds, because salary tracks the moneyline and the moneyline says nothing about scheduled length. That is always true. What makes this week unusual is that the main event is nearly even money, so neither fighter carries a favorite tax on top. You are buying sixty-seven percent more scoring time at mid-tier prices on both sides of the fight.

Compare it to a lopsided main event. When the champion is -300, the extra rounds get partly priced in through the salary and the underdog is cheap for a reason. Here the market says this is a coin flip, and the two coins both come with ten free minutes.

Our simulation makes Allen the single best piece on the slate — the highest mean score, the highest ceiling at 136.5, and the best points per dollar on the board. He appears in forty-eight percent of optimal lineups, nearly ten points clear of anyone else. Duncan is second in points per dollar.

Neither of those facts is about who wins. They are about the clock.

Why Allen specifically

Fourteen of Allen's twenty-seven wins are submissions, and he averages roughly two and a half takedown attempts per fifteen minutes. In this scoring system that combination is close to ideal: takedowns pay 5 each, control time pays 1.8 a minute, and a submission finish pays the full round bonus on top.

The decomposition is worth seeing. Across sixty thousand simulations Allen averages 24.3 points from significant strikes, 12.8 from takedowns, 7.6 from control time, and 29.3 from the win bonus — with about 254 seconds of control banked per fight. He is the only fighter on the card who earns meaningfully in four categories at once, and twenty-five minutes is why.

Duncan is the mirror image: ten of fifteen wins by knockout, 122 significant strikes across his last two fights, a four-inch reach edge, and fifty-seven percent takedown defence against Allen's forty-one percent takedown accuracy. He also has never fought past fifteen minutes. That is the whole fight in one sentence — Duncan's power against Allen's grappling, with the back half of a five-round fight as the tiebreaker.

We are on Allen, but the cheaper side is live and anyone building multiple lineups should have both.

The first-round bet

Felipe Franco finished all ten of his pre-UFC fights inside the opening five minutes. All ten.

At $9,300 and -357 he is the most expensive fighter here, and the reason to pay it is the shape of the bonus structure rather than the win probability. A first-round finish pays 90, and a finish inside sixty seconds adds another 25. Nobody else on this card carries that profile — Franco averages 58.1 points of bonus alone, which is more than some fighters score in total.

His opponent Brendson Ribeiro has the lowest floor on the slate at 3.3 points. This fight is very likely to be short and one-sided, and short and one-sided is exactly what this scoring system overpays for.

The expensive trap

Loopy Godinez is $9,000 and -244, which makes her the fourth-most expensive fighter and a clear favorite. Eleven of her fourteen wins came by decision.

That is the shape that burns tournament lineups: you pay near the top of the board for a fighter whose most likely outcome is a 30-point bonus instead of a 90-point one. Her floor is genuinely good, which makes her a reasonable cash-game piece, and our model does not hate her outright — she still projects 77 points. But she carries thirty-three percent projected ownership against twenty-seven percent of optimal lineups, and that gap is the whole argument.

Worth being precise here rather than overselling it: Godinez is not a bad fighter or a disaster play. She is a slightly negative-leverage favorite in a format that pays triple for finishing. That is a fade in a tournament and a hold in a double-up.

The one everyone will have

Niko Price is fighting for the last time. He is $7,500, +130, and he has a real knockout ceiling — our model gives him a 98-point 80th percentile, which is high for a fighter at that price.

He is also going to be popular for reasons that have nothing to do with any of that. Retirement fights draw entries. His projected ownership and his share of optimal lineups are almost identical, which means the field has him priced about right — no edge either way, just a genuinely good ceiling play that will not differentiate you.

We have him anyway. A neutral-leverage piece with a top-quartile ceiling at the bottom of the salary range is still worth owning; the differentiation has to come from somewhere else on the roster.

Where the fades are

Three fighters stand out as priced above what the simulation supports.

Julius Walker at $9,200 is the second-most expensive fighter on the card and projects 76 points, below both Franco and RJ Harris who cost about the same. Gerald Meerschaert has lost five straight and been finished in three of his last five, so the win is likely — but Walker's own output is modest, and you are paying top-tier money for a bonus you could get cheaper.

Andre Fili at $8,600 has lost three of four, two by early stoppage, and has the lowest finish lean among the mid-priced favorites. Kai Kamaka III at $7,600 is the live side, gets stronger as fights go on, and scores through volume rather than needing a finish.

RJ Harris carries the highest historical fantasy average on the slate, but on a very thin sample. We have shrunk his rates hard toward a divisional prior rather than trusting the raw number, which is the honest way to treat two or three fights of data. He is still strong. He is not as strong as the raw average implies.

What the model does and does not do

Our simulation runs sixty thousand versions of the card using verified DraftKings scoring, then evaluates rosters against five different assumptions about how the field builds. We allow one stacked fight per lineup this week, which we did not do previously, and the model now produces main-event stacks in a small share of optimal lineups — closer to what tracked professional results suggest, though still below them.

One check: tracked results show roughly seventy percent of optimal lineups contain exactly one five-round fighter, about twenty-five percent contain neither, and about five percent contain both. Our output for this card is eighty-three, fifteen and two. The skew toward having one of them is defensible on a slate where both main-event fighters rank top-two in points per dollar, but the model still under-produces the both-sides case and we are not going to pretend otherwise.

We also do not have measured ownership before lock. Nobody does. Every ownership figure here is modelled, labelled as modelled on the slate page, and stress-tested against five field shapes.

Exactly $50,000.

Allen is the engine — the clock advantage plus a grappling game that earns in every scoring category. Franco is the first-round bonus. Camilo is a solid favorite against an opponent with documented late-round chin problems. Gatto brings a brown belt and real takedown volume against a striker, and she carries positive leverage despite a long layoff. Kamaka and Price are the cheap ceilings.

This is not a contrarian build and we are not going to dress it up as one. Projected ownership across the six sums to about 215 percent, where a random six-man roster on a twenty-four-fighter slate lands near 150. We looked at lower-owned alternatives and they were worse — meaningfully worse on the number that actually matters, which is the chance of posting a winning score. Taking a large-field tournament down with one lineup is a long shot by construction; the job is to maximise that chance given what the slate offers, not to minimise the odds of sharing the prize.
