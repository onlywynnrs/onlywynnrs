UFC 331 Game Theory: Two Thirds of Winning Lineups Look the Same. The Fight Is Over Which Two.

The $500K UFC 331 Special pays $100,000 to first. That prize shape means you are not looking for the lineup that scores well on average. You are looking for the one that scores enormously and that nobody else is holding.

So we built the slate out properly. Forty thousand simulated fight outcomes — winner, method, round, strike volume, takedowns, knockdowns, and the DraftKings finish bonuses that dominate this scoring system — then ten thousand simulated tournaments across every legal roster.

Here is what came back.

The championship rounds decide this card

Two fights go five rounds: Joshua Van against Alexandre Pantoja for the flyweight title, and Arman Tsarukyan against Mauricio Ruffy in the co-main.

Because you cannot roster both sides of a fight, the most five-round fighters any lineup can hold is two. Across ten thousand simulated tournaments, the winning roster held exactly two of them 66.5 percent of the time. It held at least one 99.2 percent of the time. A lineup with zero five-round fighters won under one percent.

That is not a preference. Twenty-five minutes against fifteen is ten extra minutes of strikes, control and takedown opportunity in a format that accrues by the second, and the salary board does not charge for it. Van is $8,300 and Pantoja $7,900 — both cheaper than four fighters capped at fifteen minutes.

So the structural answer is settled before you start: take two, one from each fight. The entire game is deciding which two, and that is where the field will separate.

The sim does not pick the obvious two

Fighter presence across simulated winning lineups:

Arman Tsarukyan, 57.6 percent. Alexandre Pantoja, 51.4 percent. Joshua Van, 30.6. Mauricio Ruffy, 26.0.

Tsarukyan is unsurprising. He is a wrestler with a takedown rate near four and a half per fifteen minutes, and control time pays by the second. Give a grappler twenty-five minutes against a striker and his floor and ceiling both climb. He posted the highest ninetieth-percentile score of any fighter on the slate.

Pantoja over Van is the finding worth sitting with, because it runs against the moneyline. Van is the favorite, the champion, and the better value on paper. But the flyweight title fight rewards the man who accumulates, and Pantoja's grappling volume generates more scoring per minute than Van's striking does. Van's advantage is that he wins more often. Pantoja's advantage is that he scores more when he wins, and he costs $400 less.

In a tournament, scoring rate beats win rate. The dog is the sharper DFS side of the main event even if he is the wrong side of the bet.

The flip side: fading Ruffy is correct. He has failed to reach the third round in five of his last six fights. Against a wrestler who wants to drag him into deep water, his most likely outcome is a short night, and a short night in a five-round fight wastes the only structural edge the fight offered.

The biggest favorite is a floor play, not a ceiling play

Gable Steveson is -2000. He is 4-0 with four first-round knockouts, and he will be one of the most-rostered fighters on this card.

The sim gives him the highest mean score on the slate at 88.7, and a tenth-percentile score of 63.6 — the best floor of any fighter. That is a genuinely excellent cash-game piece.

His ninetieth percentile is 106.0. Tsarukyan's is 111.0.

That gap is the whole story. A ninety-second knockout is a wonderful result and a capped one. There is only so much striking volume and control time available in ninety seconds, and once the finish bonus is banked there is nothing left to accumulate. The fighter with the highest expected score on the slate does not have the highest ceiling, and in a tournament paying a hundred grand to first, ceiling is what you are buying.

Roster Steveson because his floor is elite and he costs a fifth of your cap. Do not roster him expecting him to win you the tournament.

Ryan Gandra and Robelis Despaigne have the same shape in cheaper form — high means, wide variance, tenth-percentile scores of 25.0 and 18.4. They finish early or they give you nothing.

Where the finishes actually are

Finish bonuses dwarf everything else in this format. A first-round win is worth ninety points. A decision is worth thirty. Getting the finishes right matters more than getting the winners right.

The fights our model has ending early: Steveson-Sharaf, Despaigne-Tuivasa, Gandra-Diaz. All three are heavy favorites against opponents with no realistic path, and all three will be popular for exactly that reason.

The fights it has going long: Yoo against Michael Aswell, who has seen five of his last six fights reach the scorecards, and the flyweight title fight, where both men have historically gone deep.

The useful distinction is between fights that finish early and generate volume first, versus fights that simply end. Doo Ho Choi arrives on a three-fight streak with all three wins by TKO and throws heavy volume on the way — he finishes after accumulating. Steveson finishes instead of accumulating. Both are good plays. Only one of them has a tournament-winning ceiling.

The control-time plays nobody prices

Takedowns are worth five points each and control time accrues continuously, which means grapplers are systematically underpriced in a format most people evaluate through knockouts.

Three fighters on this card carry meaningful takedown volume: Tsarukyan, Casey O'Neill, and Pantoja. All three appear in more than thirty percent of simulated winning lineups. O'Neill in particular is a grinder at $8,500 whose scoring does not depend on a finish at all.

Eduarda Moura is the quiet version of the same idea — real takedown volume, $7,700, and she shows up in 22 percent of winning rosters despite being nearly invisible in optimizer builds.

The under-the-radar fight

Joanderson Brito against Giga Chikadze is the fight the slate is ignoring.

Brito is a -400 favorite who projects well and generates real volume, and he appears in 28 percent of simulated winners. He shows up in a low single-digit percentage of optimizer-built lineups, because at $9,200 he trips the same cap problem every premium fighter on this slate trips.

And the public will not correct it, because Chikadze is the recognizable name in that pairing. A well-priced favorite that the modelling crowd skips on structure and the casual crowd skips on name recognition is the rarest thing you will find on a fight card.

Brunno Ferreira is the second one — 25.7 percent of simulated winners at $7,800, in a genuine coinflip against Edmen Shahbazyan where the market cannot separate the two.

The construction

Take two five-round fighters, one from each fight. The sim says this two thirds of the time and the salary board makes it free.

Make them Tsarukyan and Pantoja unless you have a specific read that says otherwise. Both outscore their opponents per minute even accounting for winning less often.

Add one premium finisher for the floor, not for the ceiling. Steveson if you can afford him, Despaigne or Gandra if you cannot.

Take a grappler. O'Neill or Moura, because takedown volume scores whether or not anything gets finished.

Find your leverage in Brito, not in the minimum-priced punts. Sean Sharaf and Ozzy Diaz carry real ownership in optimizer builds and are both facing men who finish in the opening round. That is ownership without upside, which is the worst combination available.

And never put both sides of a fight in the same lineup.
