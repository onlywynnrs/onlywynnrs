UFC 331: Everyone Is Telling You to Stack the Title Fight. We Simulated It 12,000 Times.

The $500K UFC 331 Special pays $100,000 to first. Twelve fights, six roster spots, fifty thousand dollars.

Read the public breakdowns this week and one instruction appears in nearly all of them: stack the main event. Take both Joshua Van and Alexandre Pantoja. It is a title fight, the odds are close, both men are action fighters, and the pricing is friendly at $8,300 and $7,900.

We simulated forty thousand versions of this card and twelve thousand tournaments across every legal roster construction. That advice loses, and it loses badly.

The stack is 19% of the lineups and 1% of the winners

DraftKings permits rostering both sides of a fight, so the question is strategic rather than legal. Here is what came back.

Builds containing both Van and Pantoja made up 18.9 percent of the viable lineup pool. They won 1.2 percent of simulated tournaments.

Widen it to any stacked fight and the gap gets worse. Stacked builds were 68.8 percent of the candidate pool and won 6.3 percent of the time. Ninety-three point six percent of winning lineups contained no stack at all.

The reason is not complicated once you see it. Two fighters in one bout split the same minutes, and one of them loses. The loser gets no win bonus, lands fewer strikes, and scores at a fraction of the winner's rate. Our sim has Pantoja's median night at 42.4 points and Van's at 79.8 — because Pantoja loses roughly 55 percent of the time. Rostering both is paying full price for a slot that is guaranteed to underperform in every single simulation.

Stacking is a cash-game tool. It locks in a floor when you are trying to beat half the field. In a top-heavy tournament with a hundred grand on top, the floor is not what you are buying.

That the advice is everywhere is precisely why it matters. It is not just wrong, it is wrong and crowded.

Take one. Which one is closer than the price suggests.

Van appears in 51.5 percent of simulated winning lineups. Pantoja appears in 43.4.

Van lands just under 8.5 significant strikes per minute, which is elite volume, and across twenty-five minutes that produces a genuinely high median. Pantoja lands roughly 2.8 takedowns per fifteen minutes and is the better grappler, which produces a lower median and a comparable ceiling — their ninetieth percentiles are 104.2 and 103.2, essentially identical.

So you are choosing between a higher floor at $8,300 and an identical ceiling at $7,900, with the $400 saved buying you real flexibility elsewhere. Van is the correct cash play. Pantoja is the correct tournament play if you need the salary, and the sim does not strongly separate them.

What it does separate is the idea of taking both.

The scoring system is not about knockouts

This is the part most casual entrants never internalise, and it drives everything above.

A striker who wins a competitive decision and lands a hundred significant strikes scores roughly seventy points. That is a good fight and a losing fantasy night — it will not reach an optimal lineup. Strikers score through significant strikes and knockdowns, and that is close to the whole menu.

Grapplers score through takedowns at five points each, advancing position, control time that accrues continuously, and submission attempts, plus the same strikes and the same win bonus. They have more ways to reach a big number and they reach it without needing anything dramatic to happen.

Which is why Arman Tsarukyan appears in 66.2 percent of simulated winning lineups — the highest share of any fighter on this card by a wide margin. He takes down opponents about four and a half times per fifteen minutes, he is a heavy favorite, and he has twenty-five minutes to accumulate. His ninetieth percentile is 128 and his ninety-ninth is 152, both the best on the slate.

He is the closest thing to a mandatory play here, and the published breakdowns agree, which means you will not be alone. That is fine. Sometimes the correct play is the popular one and your edge has to come from the other five slots.

Where the writing is, and where it isn't

The names appearing in nearly every public breakdown this week: Tsarukyan, Van, Doo Ho Choi over Patricio Pitbull, Robelis Despaigne over Tai Tuivasa, Iwo Baraniewski finishing Alonzo Menifield quickly.

Those are defensible reads. They will also be well represented.

The interesting part is the fighter the breakdowns are unanimously against.

Mauricio Ruffy is being faded everywhere. The stated reasoning is sound — he is a +284 underdog, Tsarukyan's wrestling is the worst possible matchup for him, and he is unlikely to score much in a loss.

Our sim has him in 28.4 percent of winning lineups, and he only wins the fight a quarter of the time.

That is not a contradiction. It is the definition of a tournament play. Ruffy has stopped Rafael Fiziev and battered Michael Chandler this year. He has failed to reach the third round in five of his last six fights, which means his outcomes are bimodal — he gets finished, or he finishes. When he finishes early he banks a ninety-point round-one bonus at a $7,100 salary, and his ninetieth percentile lands at 92.2 despite that terrible win probability.

He will be rostered by almost nobody, because every public source told people not to. Taking Ruffy means giving up Tsarukyan, which is a real cost. But if you want one genuine swing on this card, that is where the field has vacated entirely.

Three more nobody is writing about

Brunno Ferreira shows up in 28.5 percent of simulated winners at $7,800. He is in a coinflip against Edmen Shahbazyan where the market cannot separate the two, and he finishes when he wins.

Eduarda Moura, 21.7 percent, at $7,700. Real takedown volume — the scoring avenue that pays without needing a finish — in a fight nobody is previewing.

Alonzo Menifield, 21.0 percent, at $7,500. The public read is that Baraniewski finishes him quickly. He is a +197 dog who finishes 60 percent of his own wins, and he is the cheapest route to affording a premium fighter.

Joanderson Brito at 28.8 percent is the last one. He is a -400 favorite who went on a five-fight run of consecutive stoppages, and the recognizable name in his fight is Giga Chikadze, which is where casual money will go.

The favorite with the capped ceiling

Gable Steveson carries the highest mean score on the slate at 92.7 and the best median at 97.0. He is -2000 and finishes in the first round.

His ninetieth percentile is 116.0. Tsarukyan's is 128.0. His ninety-ninth is 134.0 against Tsarukyan's 152.4.

A ninety-second knockout banks ninety bonus points and then the scoring stops. There is no volume left to accumulate, no control time, no second act. Steveson is the best floor on this card and the wrong place to look for the score that wins a hundred thousand dollars.

Play him because he is safe and cheap relative to what he returns. Do not play him expecting the top of the leaderboard.

Construction

One fighter from each fight. No stacks — 93.6 percent of simulated winners had none.

Tsarukyan unless you are deliberately taking the Ruffy swing. He is in two thirds of winning lineups and there is no clever way around that.

One of Van or Pantoja, not both. Van for floor, Pantoja for the $400.

Prioritise takedown volume over striking volume at equal salary. The scoring system pays grapplers more ways than it pays strikers, and the public evaluates fighters through knockouts.

Find your differentiation below $8,000 — Ferreira, Moura, Menifield — rather than in the minimum-priced punts. Sean Sharaf and Ozzy Diaz carry real optimizer ownership and both face men who finish in round one. That is ownership with no upside attached, which is the worst thing you can put in a lineup.
