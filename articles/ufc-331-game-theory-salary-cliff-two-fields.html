UFC 331 Game Theory: Two Different Fields Are Building Two Different Lineups

The $500K UFC 331 Special pays $100,000 to first. That prize shape means the lineup you want is not the one that scores best on average — it is the one that scores enormously and that almost nobody else is holding.

So the question worth answering is where everyone else will be. And on this slate there is a clean, mechanical answer.

The best value on the board appears in 2% of lineups

Run RotoWire's projections through their own optimizer for 150 lineups and Gable Steveson shows up in three of them.

He is the second-best value play on the card. Ninety-nine projected points at $9,900 is 10.08 per thousand dollars, behind only Joshua Van. The optimizer produced him twice in a hundred tries.

It is not an error, and it is not an opinion about Steveson. It is arithmetic, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.

The salary cliff

Six roster spots and a $50,000 cap means an average of $8,333 per fighter. Now look at where value lives on this slate.

Every fighter priced between $7,700 and $9,000 returns between 7.1 and 10.6 points per thousand. The group averages 9.20.

Every fighter priced at $7,500 or below returns between 3.4 and 5.63. That group averages 4.95.

There is no gradual decline. There is a cliff, and it sits between Eduarda Moura at $7,700 and Alonzo Menifield at $7,500. Above it, roughly nine points per thousand. Below it, roughly five.

That structure dictates everything. To afford a $9,900 fighter you must buy from the cheap tier to balance the cap, and the cheap tier is where value collapses. An optimizer maximizing projected points will simply refuse. It fills all six slots from the band where value is uniformly high and never touches the top or the bottom.

Which is exactly what happened. Casey O'Neill appears in 69 percent of those lineups. Iwo Baraniewski 68. Joshua Van 59. Edmen Shahbazyan 57. Charles Jourdain 55. Doo Ho Choi 45.

We enumerated all 42,185 legal lineups on this slate independently. In the top fifty by projection, O'Neill appears 84 percent of the time, Van 82, Baraniewski 80. Two different tools, same six names. That is not consensus. That is a constraint.

The cost of breaking it is smaller than it looks

The maximum-projection lineup on this slate scores 459.2. Its cumulative exposure is 312 percent, and by our duplication index it is one of the most-shared constructions available.

The best lineup containing Steveson scores 446.8. That is 12.4 points worse, or 2.7 percent.

Its duplication index is 55 times lower.

Two and a half percent of projected points for a fifty-five-fold reduction in the chance you split $100,000. In a top-heavy tournament that is not a close call.

The part everyone will get wrong

Here is the caveat that matters, and it is the reason we are not simply telling you to jam Steveson.

RotoWire's exposure is not DraftKings' ownership. It is what the optimizer-using slice of the field will build — and that slice is small. The rest of a $500K special is casual entrants, and casual entrants in UFC DFS do one thing reliably: they roster the biggest favorite on the card.

Gable Steveson is a -2000 favorite with four professional wins, all first-round knockouts, against a heavyweight on a two-fight losing streak. His actual ownership will not be 2 percent. It will be substantial.

Same story for Robelis Despaigne at -700 against Tai Tuivasa, who has lost seven straight UFC fights.

So there are genuinely two fields here building two different rosters, and the truth sits between them. The sharp crowd fades the expensive favorites because of the cap. The public hammers them because of the moneyline.

Where the real leverage is

The best spots are the fighters that both fields ignore, and there are four of them.

Joanderson Brito is the clearest. He is a -400 favorite, projects 84.3 points, and returns 9.17 per thousand — genuinely good. He appears in 7 percent of optimizer lineups because $9,200 trips the cap problem. And the casual field will gravitate to Giga Chikadze in that fight, because Chikadze is the recognizable name. A well-priced favorite that the sharps skip on structure and the public skips on name recognition is the rarest thing on a slate.

Alonzo Menifield at 1 percent and Marlon Vera at 2 percent are cheap-tier fighters with genuinely poor value ratios, which is why nothing rosters them. But they are the two cheapest routes to affording a premium fighter, and Vera at +182 against Jourdain is a live underdog rather than a dead one.

Eduarda Moura at 3 percent sits just above the cliff at $7,700 with a 7.10 ratio — the last fighter before value falls off, and almost unowned.

What we are not doing is chasing Sean Sharaf at 21 percent or Ozzy Diaz at 19 percent. Those are the punts the optimizer reaches for when it does take a premium fighter, which means they carry optimizer ownership without carrying any upside. Both are facing men who finish in the first round.

The construction

Take Joshua Van. He is the single best value on the slate at 10.58, he fights five rounds while most of this card fights three, and even at 59 percent exposure the price is simply correct.

Take exactly one premium fighter the optimizer refuses to buy — Steveson or Brito, not both unless you are willing to punt twice.

Pay for the punt from the 1-to-3 percent group rather than the 19-to-21 percent group. Menifield and Vera cost the same as Diaz and Sharaf in salary and a fraction as much in ownership.

And keep both sides of any fight out of the lineup. Always.
