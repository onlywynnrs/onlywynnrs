UFC 329 DraftKings Salaries Breakdown: Best Values & Lineup Builds

UFC 329 Salaries Are Out — And the Pricing Tells a Story

DraftKings released the UFC 329 salaries, and the structure is unusual enough that the pricing itself is the strategy. On most cards, the biggest favorite is the most expensive fighter and the price ladder roughly follows the odds. This card breaks that in three places — and each break is an opportunity. Here's the salary-by-salary read for Saturday's McGregor vs. Holloway 2 slate, using the actual DK numbers.

The Top Shelf: $9,900–$9,000

Gable Steveson ($9,900) is the most expensive fighter on the slate, and at -2800 he's the closest thing to a guaranteed win DFS pricing allows. But $9.9K is nearly a fifth of your $50K cap for a debut heavyweight with zero UFC scoring history. The market's under 1.5 rounds is heavily juiced, so a fast finish is the expectation — if he delivers it, he's worth every dollar. If he wins a grinding decision, you paid top-shelf price for a mid-shelf score. That's the whole Steveson bet in one line.

Farid Basharat ($9,600) at 15-0 against a short-notice replacement is the quiet chalk we flagged in our full-card breakdown — an 86.4 career scoring average and the cleanest win-probability-per-dollar in the premium tier.

Adrian Yanez ($9,400) and Luke Riley ($9,300) round out the finisher chalk. Yanez's 68.6 average is modest for the price, but the matchup (Garbrandt's compromised durability) is the draw. Riley is 13-0 with a 79.9 average — the field will be all over him.

Max Holloway ($9,000) is the pricing anomaly of the slate. He's a -225 favorite in a five-round main event with an 86.2 scoring average — and he costs less than Costa, Riley, Yanez, Basharat, and Steveson. Five-round fights carry roughly 25 minutes of scoring runway, and Holloway's volume is historic. At $9K, the market is effectively discounting him for the McGregor circus. This is the best structural value in the premium tier, full stop.

The Mid-Range Engine Room: $8,900–$8,300

This band is where the slate's projected points actually live, and two numbers jump off the page:

Damian Pinas ($8,900) carries a 118.9 scoring average — the second-highest on the board — at a -218 price. Ryan Gandra ($8,600) tops everyone at 130.6. Those are massive per-fight averages from early-card fighters most casual entrants will scroll past on their way to the main event. Small samples, yes (both are 9-1), and the honest caveat is that averages built on a few fights can mislead. But at these salaries, they're the engine-room plays that let you afford Steveson or Holloway up top.

Benoit Saint Denis ($8,500) at 101.0 average in the co-main — where the round total screams finish — is priced like a mid-tier option and profiles like a premium one. Terrance McKinney ($8,400) brings his minutes-not-rounds chaos at a fair price. Cory Sandhagen ($8,300) is the decision-risk we keep flagging: an 89.2 average, but bantamweight chess against Bautista can go fifteen quiet minutes.

The Leverage Band: $7,900–$7,200

Paddy Pimblett ($7,700, 93.0 avg) is the underdog with the best scoring profile in the band — in the fight the market expects to end early. Both-sides exposure to the co-main is the play across tournament entries.

Nikita Krylov ($7,500) almost never sees a scorecard — an all-finish profile against Whittaker at a salary that leaves you $2,500 per remaining slot above average. Mario Bautista ($7,900) offers the contrarian decision-floor angle: his wrestling pace racks up takedown points even without a finish.

Conor McGregor ($7,200, 88.2 avg) is the fascinating one. That scoring average is from an era when he was one of the sport's elite finishers — it tells you what prime McGregor did, not what a 37-year-old returning from five years away will do. He'll be the most-owned underdog on the slate by a mile, because casual money rosters the name. At +185, his ownership will dwarf his real win probability. We said it in the full breakdown and the salary confirms it: he's a negative-leverage play. If he wins, you win with half the field. If Holloway wins, the field craters and you didn't.

The Punt Bin: $7,100 and Below

Cody Garbrandt ($6,800) is the only punt with genuine one-punch upside — a former champion's power at a bottom-shelf price. Kai Kamaka III ($6,900) and Cody Durden ($7,100) are salary relief with modest ceilings. John Garza ($6,600), the short-notice debutant, and Elisha Ellison ($6,300) are the "unlock two studs" buttons — but Ellison's 1.6 scoring average against a -2800 Olympian is as close to a guaranteed zero as DFS offers. Punting him means betting five fighters can carry six seats.

Three Builds From This Structure

The Balanced Build: Holloway ($9.0K) + Basharat ($9.6K) anchors, Pinas/Gandra engine room, one co-main side, one punt with a pulse (Garbrandt). Uses the Holloway discount without the Steveson tax.

The Steveson Build: Eat the $9,900, then you need the engine room — Gandra and Pinas become near-mandatory to stay under cap, and your last seats hunt finishes in the leverage band (Krylov, Pimblett).

The Full Leverage Build (tournaments only): Fade Steveson entirely, roster Holloway + Yanez + the highest-average mid-range, and take Krylov/Bautista/Green as your separators. If Steveson grinds a decision instead of a fast finish, this build passes 80% of the field at once.

The honest close: salary structure tells you where the field will be, not who wins fights. Steveson at $9,900 and McGregor's name-brand ownership will concentrate the field in predictable places. The edge on this slate isn't picking different winners — it's paying different prices for the same probabilities. Holloway at $9,000 is the card's clearest example.
