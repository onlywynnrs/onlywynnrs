UFC 329 DFS Strategy: McGregor vs Holloway Leverage, Chalk & Picks

UFC 329 Is a Trap Card Dressed as a Party

McGregor vs. Holloway 2 headlines International Fight Week, and the DFS fields for this card will be enormous — casual money floods in whenever McGregor fights. That is the single most important strategic fact of the weekend. Big soft fields reward discipline: the DraftKings scoring still pays finishes above everything (a first-round finish is worth +90, a decision just +30), and the casual money will be distributed by name recognition, not by finish probability. Your edge this week is playing the scoring system while the field plays the storylines.

We don't sell certainty — fights are chaos, and this card has more genuine unknowns than most. Here is how we're reading it.

The Main Event Is an Ownership Problem, Not a Pick Problem

Holloway opened as high as -550 when the rematch was booked. Heavy public money on McGregor's name has dragged the line all the way to roughly -225, with McGregor around +185. Read that carefully: the market's sharp side has been letting the public shorten McGregor's price for weeks. A five-year layoff, a 1-3 run since 2018, a broken leg in his last outing, and a move up to welterweight — the line move toward him is sentiment, not information.

For DFS, that cuts two ways. McGregor will carry enormous casual ownership regardless of price — he's the most rostered name on the slate by default. If he lands the left hand early, everyone has him and you gain nothing. If Holloway does what the opening line implied — high-volume, durable, drowning a rusty fighter over five rounds — the McGregor-heavy field collapses and Holloway lineups take the tournament. Holloway is the rare spot where the favorite is the leverage play, because the crowd is on the dog. One caveat: Holloway's most likely win is late or by decision (his decision prop is +440, KO -125), which caps his ceiling — he's a leverage anchor, not a slate-breaker.

The Co-Main Is Where the Finish Equity Lives

Benoit Saint Denis vs. Paddy Pimblett is the fight the scoring system loves. Saint Denis is around -131 to -142, riding a four-fight finishing streak, and the under 2.5 rounds is juiced to -160 — the market is openly telling you it expects a finish. Pimblett is coming off his first UFC loss and has been hittable early in fights his whole career. Saint Denis is a legitimate tournament anchor: a modest favorite with real early-finish probability. Pimblett at plus money is the contrarian dart only if you believe his durability and late scrambles show up again — but understand you're betting against what the round market is screaming.

The Quiet Chalk and the Real Leverage Spots

Gable Steveson (-2800) makes his debut against Elisha Ellison, with the under 1.5 rounds at -600. An Olympic gold medalist wrestler being fed a debut opponent is the most projectable quick finish on the card — he'll be the highest-owned fighter on the slate and probably deserves it. The honest caveat: he has zero UFC scoring history, and a -2800 favorite who grinds instead of finishing is how chalk disappoints. He belongs in most lineups; he wins you nothing alone.

Adrian Yanez (-410) vs. Cody Garbrandt is the cleaner finish-chalk — Garbrandt has been stopped repeatedly, and Yanez hunts knockouts.

Terrance McKinney (-140) vs. King Green might be the best points-per-dollar fight on the board. McKinney's fights barely ever see round two in either direction — he owns some of the fastest finishes in UFC history and gets finished fast when it goes wrong. That volatility is exactly what tournaments pay for.

Robert Whittaker (-142) vs. Nikita Krylov at light heavyweight is a veteran-vs-veteran spot where Whittaker's consistency profiles more like a decision — solid floor, modest ceiling.

Brandon Royval (+185) vs. Lone'er Kavanagh is the sneaky leverage fight: Kavanagh is a hyped -220 favorite coming off a highlight knockout, but Royval is a ranked, chaotic flyweight who has never been in a boring fight. A live, proven dog against a prospect the field will over-trust — that's the classic tournament pivot.

Wang Cong vs. Tracy Cortez is a genuine pick'em (-112/-108). Coin-flip fights are ownership dead zones; whichever side you take, you're getting leverage almost for free.

Building for a Massive Casual Field

The framework this week: anchor with the projectable finishers (Steveson, Yanez, Saint Denis), take your volatility swing on McKinney, and make your main-event call a leverage decision, not a fandom decision — which likely means more Holloway than the field will have. Fade the temptation to build the all-name-value lineup (McGregor + Pimblett + Garbrandt); that's exactly the lineup ten thousand casuals will click in, and it's built on the three most fragile spots on the card.

And a word we'll keep repeating: nobody knows what five years off looks like on a 37-year-old McGregor. Anyone selling certainty on this main event is selling you a story. We read the market, weigh the finish math, and show our work.
